## Introduction


The production of cider and perry is a traditional fermentation process similar to wine making, which involves using microorganisms (primarily yeasts) to convert apple or pear juice into the final product (Figure 1). For the whole process – from fruit reception into the “Cider Mill” (the place where cider or perry is made) through to dispatch of the final product (e.g. packaged in a glass bottle) some months later – a range of specialised equipment is used. In addition to apples and pears, other ingredients (e.g. sugar for a sweet cider) are also often incorporated to make cider or perry. The production of perry is essentially the same as cider making (other than the fruit used) therefore the term “cider” will be used throughout this section to describe the production process for these two beverages. Where there are differences, these will be highlighted. On a simple level – as carried out by some cider makers – the whole process may only involve the following steps, which are kept as simple as possible and with minimal intervention by the cider maker: 1. Fruit selection & harvesting 2. Milling & pressing 3. Fermentation of the juice using ‘wild’ indigenous yeasts at ambient temperature 4. (Racking) 5. Maturation of post-fermented cider 6. (Blending & packaging) Some very good ciders can be made in this way, but by just letting ‘nature’ drive and steer the whole process, the outcome will typically be highly unpredictable. As cider making is a microbiological process, a number of microbiological control techniques can also be utilized. Arguably, this is the key function of the cider maker, whose job is to select and implement the appropriate microbial control techniques. It also should also be recognised that, in apples, all the constituent sugars, which make up about 80% of the soluble solids in apple juice, are fully fermentable by yeasts and thus a fully attenuated cider will be completely dry and devoid of all sweetness. For most individuals this “dry” cider can be quite a challenging drink! (Note: many pears typically contain un-fermentable sugar in the form of sorbitol, although this ‘sugar-alcohol’ is a laxative and some individuals are very sensitive to its effects!). Essentially, both cider and perry are drinks to be enjoyed by their consumers, thus all cider makers also need to carefully consider what product attributes their consumers require (e.g. clear vs. cloudy cider, dry vs. sweet cider etc.). In respect to this, cider making can thus be viewed as a two-stage process: 1. From fruit selection through to the post-fermentation maturation. The Prime objective of this stage is to produce high quality (so-called) “Base Ciders”, which are the main ingredients for subsequent … 2. …Downstream processing & packaging – This stage includes blending, clarification, product make-up and packaging. The Prime objective of this stage is to meet consumer requirements. It is often said that “cider making is all in the blending”!

## Principles of Production


The production of cider is based on four key and interlinked principles: 1. Appropriate choice and care of equipment; 2. Microbiological control; 3. Maintenance of high standards of hygiene; 4. Quality Assurance. Principle 1: Equipment Selection and Care Materials Used Cider and apple juice is corrosive to:Lead, iron, copper, zinc, cadmium & aluminum. Many of the compounds formed by contact with these metals are poisonous and therefore all modern day producers are careful to avoid any use of these materials in the cider making process. Up until the latter part of the 18th century many people did get sick from consuming cider contaminated with poisonous metals - the problem used to be called “Devonshire Colic”. English physician George Baker first identified the cause in 1767 after examining the symptoms and records of over 300 individuals taken sick into Exeter hospital. He also found that some cider presses were lined with sheets of lead. After these findings, cider producers changed the materials used in their cider making equipment and the consumer can now be assured that cider is one of the safest food and drink products available. Apple juice and cider should, therefore, only come into contact with the following:

- Food grade “316” stainless steel,

- Food grade plastics,

- Non-resinous wood - Oak, Ash and Chestnut,

- Special food grade epoxy-resin linings (e.g. in aluminum drink cans),

- Glass.

It should also be noted that juice & cider are corrosive to concrete, thus consideration should be given to including a resin coat on the floor of a production facility. Key Items of Equipment Used A range of equipment is used in the cider making process, but items will vary between different producers as determined by their production scale and the specific type of processing utilized. For example, some producers will send all their raw cider for final processing and bottling to a contract packer. The main types of equipment can be grouped as follows:

- Fruit handling equipment e.g. elevators, fruit washers.

- Fruit mill – used to grind-up the hard apples and pears into a mash prior to pressing.

- Cider press – used to extract juice from the fruit mash.

- Fermentation vats & cider storage vessels. A wide range of different sizes and styles can be found in the

cider industry – from small wooden barrels (freshly used rum barrels are prized by many producers) and oak vats, through to large temperature-controlled stainless-steel tanks. It is interesting to note that the largest fermentation and storage tank in the world – across all types of alcoholic drinks – can be found at Bulmers in Hereford, UK. It has a capacity of just under 7.3 million liters!

- Pumps & pipe-work. It is very important to note that there are two main types of pumps – “Positive

Displacement” (e.g. flexible impellor; typically used for bulk liquid transfers) and “Centrifugal” (typically used to run filters, fillers and for tank cleaning). Although it is quite feasible for a cider maker themselves to source the former type of pump (as long as its capacity is defined), it is strongly recommended that expert specialist advice is sought when buying centrifugal pumps as they can cavitate if not used correctly and cause severe damage to the pump itself as well as to the cider . Simply, a centrifugal pump should be directly matched and sized to the specific pumping requirement identified (“Duty”).

- Clarification equipment e.g. filters designed to produce a clear cider (NB some cider is sold as being cloudy).

- Stabilisation equipment e.g. a pasteuriser – used to ensure the product will not spoil after it is bottled.

- Packaging equipment e.g. a bottle filler.

To minimize the risk of the cider being spoiled, all equipment will be thoroughly sanitized (cleaned and treated to ensure that microbial contamination is prevented) before use. Cider makers should also undertake regular maintenance to ensure that equipment does not break down during its use. Principle 2: Microbiological Control The key aim during the production of cider is to encourage the positive benefits of certain microorganisms – notably fermentation and the production of alcohol by a yeast (species of Saccharomyces) - and to prevent the negative effects of others – for example, the production of acetic acid (vinegar) by Acetic Acid bacteria (notably, species of Acetomonas). The Cider Maker uses a number of strategies to implement this principle: 1) Ensuring high standards of hygiene – the use of sanitized equipment and clean fruit will restrict the numbers of unwanted microorganisms present. Microbiology is a ‘numbers game’; the more microorganisms present the greater effect they will have (see below – Principle 3). 2) Encouraging microbial competition and dominance – sufficient numbers of the selected fermentation yeast will ensure that other unwanted organisms have a limited ability to grow and exert their effects during the production of cider. This is mainly due to competition for nutrients. Unfortunately, this ‘tool’ for microbial control can only be used during juice fermentation (and possibly during any malo-lactic fermentation). 3) Ensuring appropriate conditions during cider production – this includes the maintenance of correct pH (below 3.8), oxygen control and temperature control. A key action is to ensure that anaerobic conditions (absence of oxygen) are maintained during fermentation and maturation e.g. by the use of airlocks. Many unwanted microorganisms are aerobic (e.g. Acetic Acid bacteria) and will only grow in the presence of oxygen. 4) Using anti-microbial agents and other technological methods – the main ‘tool’ that Cider Makers have used and continue to use is Sulphur Dioxide (SO2), which suppresses many of the unwanted yeasts and bacteria. Other technologies are available, however, including pasteurisation and sterile filtration. Principle 3: Maintenance of High Standards of Hygiene As introduced above, the maintenance of a high standard of hygiene is critical to the production of good quality cider. Cider producers must pay particular attention to this principle and invest in appropriate resources to ensure that high standards are achieved throughout all stages of the production process. A typical sanitization routine is usually a two-stage process and will include: 1. Cleaning ~ Physical removal of unwanted (mainly organic) material, which typically involves: q Hand scrubbing q Pressure washing q Use of Cleaning-In-Place (CIP) systems for tanks and pipe-lines q Use of hot water and detergents 2. Disinfection ~ Reduction of micro-organisms to an acceptable level, which typically involves: q Use of steam or very hot water q Use of selected ‘food-safe’ chemicals (“Terminal Disinfectants”).

Principle 4: Quality Assurance Traditionally a Quality Control (QC) approach was taken, which typically involved inspection of ingredients, inspection of the process and, last but not least, an inspection of the final products. Then, if anything fails to meet specification, it is rejected. Although inspection does have a role to play in the cider industry (inspection of apples on arrival to the mill, for example) in many cases it is significantly more cost-effective – and more appropriate – to take a proactive and preventative approach: Quality Assurance (QA). This involves the following: 1. There is a systematic, detailed pre-production assessment of the product – i.e. all its ingredients and all the processes used to make the product in order to identify anything that could go wrong and thus cause a consumer health problem … and/or… a problem with the quality of the product … and/or…a problem with the efficiency and effectiveness of the process. 2. The producer then develops, implements and operates cost-effective controls and procedures that aim to ensure that the cider produced will be safe, of good quality and manufactured in an efficient and effective manner. As detailed above, Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point (HACCP) is the most powerful QA tool available; underpinned by Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP). Although – strictly – HACCP is used to assure consumer safety, the principles and process can be (and often are) adapted to product quality and process efficiency & effectiveness. In addition to HACCP , cider companies will also have other Quality Management Systems (QMS) in place, such as ISO 9001 and ISO 14001.
