## A. background to cider & perry production



## History of the Cider Industry



## Apple and Pear Origins


The history of cider and perry cannot be dissociated from that of the production of its basic ingredients – apples and pears. When our ancestors started to grow the fruit for use as a staple food it would be inevitable that, at some early stage, fermented beverages of one form or another would have also been produced from the raw materials. As early as 6500 BC, sweet eating apples of the species Malus sieversii, cultivated and originating from Tien Shan (“Mountains of Heaven”), began to spread east and west along the trade routes into China, India and the Middle East. Indeed Almaty, one of the main cities in the Tien Shan region of Kazakhstan, means “Father of Apples” – in recognition of the eating apple’s beginnings in this part of the world. Along with Asian traders, Persian, Greek and Roman conquerors, early Christian monks and European explorers were also all involved in moving this sweet eating apple westward. The small, acidic and harsh crab apple (Malus sylvestris) is, however, native not only to Asia but also to the more western and northern latitudes including Britain and other areas of Europe. The early inhabitants of Western Europe would have made use of this fruit until the more pleasant apple from Tien Shan reached them. There is evidence, from a Neolithic site in Wiltshire UK, for the use of M. sylvestris as a food by ancient Britons about 5,000 years ago. Hybrids of the two main apple species began to appear which would have also been crossed with the bitter Malus orientalis from the Caucasus and Malus pumila, another wild apple species found throughout Europe and central Asia, to produce the many different varieties that eventually became established. There are now known to be more than 7,500 cultivars of apples. Pears are “pome” fruits and as such are related to apples. Like apple trees, pear trees have origins in north-central Asia but are also native to temperate regions of the world, from Western Europe, North Africa and throughout the whole of Asia. They are thought to have been preferred over apples by the ancient Chinese, Greeks, and Romans. Cultivated for over 3,000 years, pears for eating were popularized in Europe by in the 17th Century by Louis XIV of France as his favourite fruit. Overall, there are about 30 species of pears and thousands of cultivars. Three are important for edible fruit production, the European Pear Pyrus communis cultivated mainly in Europe and North America, the Ya Pear Pyrus bretschneideri, and the Nashi (Asian) Pear Pyrus pyrifolia, both grown mainly in eastern Asia. The wild pears, Pyrus communis and Pyrus nivalis, indigenous to Europe, were the main species originally cultivated for the production of perry centuries ago. Apples and Pears for Food and Drink – The Origins of Cider and Perry By 1000 BC, apples and a range of processed apple products (including sauces and syrups and certainly also fermented apple juice) became a standard part of the diet in the Mediterranean world. The Romans were key in spreading the knowledge of apples and their cultivation and use throughout the regions they conquered. However, they would have also adopted other knowledge and practices found in the regions they settled in and from the other cultures and peoples they met during their travels. When early Roman invaders arrived in England around 55 B.C, they came across native Britons in Kent drinking something like cider made of apples. Records testify that the Romans and especially their commander Julius Caesar enthusiastically adopted the practice. Nobody knows how long the local population had been producing this drink before the Romans appeared. It is also known that Hebrews were drinking Shekar (“A strong drink”) and Greeks were drinking Sikera – a kind of a drink that was made by boiling apples and fermenting the juice. The Greek geographer Strabo (64 BC to 23 AD) described a great number of apples and pears that had been cultivated in Gaul and he hinted of the drink called “Phitarra” found in the land of the Basques (southern France and northern Spain), which was made by boiling pieces of apples in water with honey. Later, there are records of pear wine being made by the Romans in the 4th century AD and at that time Saint Gerome actually mentions perry (“Piracium”) – the first author to introduce the word into the Latin language. It was during the reign of Charlemagne - “Emperor of the Holy Roman Empire” - from AD 742 to 814 that the production of cider (“Pomacium”) and perry became properly established. At this time the products were typically made, as elsewhere (including Britain), by steeping slices of fruit (primarily crab apples and wild pears) in water and allowing the “depense” to ferment.

In Britain, after Christianity was introduced in AD 597 by St. Augustine, orchards were planted in monasteries and cider was made, although it was not until after the Norman conquest in 1066 that apple growing and cider making commenced in a significant way. But there had also been some ‘movement’ the other way, for example, during the 6th century Saint Teilo of Wales had taken apples used to make cider across to Normandy to establish an orchard there – providing additional evidence of a long established Celtic tradition of cider making. It should be remembered that the Celtic Britons came to Britain long before the Romans first arrived and it is likely that they were the original producers of cider in this country. However, evidence seems to suggest that the specific cultivation and selection of fruit for the purposes of cider making, along with some of the original technologies (e.g. olive mills and presses which allowed juice to be properly extracted from apples) came from east to west into Christendom up through Moorish Spain. It’s worth noting that most of the traditional varieties of apples suitable for cider are very late in ripening, which is indicative of their origin in a warmer climate. By the 12 th century there is good evidence of the well- established cultivation of cider apples and cider making technology in the Basque region of Spain. After the Norman Conquest, cider production became well established in the monasteries of Britain – particularly, to start with, in Kent, Hampshire, Sussex and Somerset. In these early days of the industry, the public bought most of their cider from the monasteries. In medieval times, cider making was an important industry in Kent and continued to grow in importance across France and other countries of Europe as well – in the 14 th century cider became a serious rival to beer and wine at inns. In 1371 in Cannes, for instance, sales of ciders were almost equal to those of wines. Cider making had its heyday in the 17th century, when great attention was paid to the apple varieties used and the quality of the resulting products. The development of perry production followed a similar pattern, although became mainly restricted to the area around present day Gloucestershire, Herefordshire, Monmouthshire and Worcestershire. It is likely that the perry pear trees were also brought into Britain by the Norman invaders becoming primarily established in these western counties.


## Historical Development of Cider in North America


This development of the industry in North America can be illustrated by referencing a number of key milestones as follows:

- In 1617, the first apple tree was planted by Louis Hebert in Quebec City, New France, three years earlier than that

first recorded in New England, USA.

- There is good evidence to suggest that in 1620 Puritans on the Mayflower took with them cider making equipment

and apples trees to the “New World”. A few days after the ship cider arrived in North America, William Blackstone planted the first apple trees in the New England colony. Furthermore, cider was taken to Newfoundland from Exmouth as an anti scurvy measure – as stated in 1626 by Francis Bacon “Cider and perry are notable beverages in long sea voyages. A wonderful and refreshing drink…an assured remedy for sickness taken at sea…greeting those ill with scurvy to be given cider and oranges as a instant cure upon landing”.

- In 1634 the Maryland settlers were encouraged to ship-in “kernalls of peares and apples for maykinge thereafter

cider and perry”.

- The first known apple and pear nursery was established in 1648 in Massachusetts and large-scale propagation of

fruit for cider production occurred to supply local taverns with the drink.

- By 1660, due to the problems caused by excessive consumption of cider, regulations were introduced in the

Massachusetts Bay Colony, Maryland and Virginia to control consumption and distribution. Court records exist detailing the fines levied for drunkenness on cider.

- In Canada in the mid 17th century, the country’s earliest dedicated cider brewery was established in Mont Royal in

New France, by Sulpician priests, although much production had previously existed and continued as a farmhouse produced drink.

- In 1678, the Secretary of the Virginia House of Burgesses is recorded as blaming recent riots on excess cider

consumption: “All plantations flowing with syder, soe unripe drank by our licentious inhabitants, that they allow no tyme for its fermentation but in their braines”.

- In 1682 it was recorded “At Newark is made great quantities of syder, exseeding any wee have in New England,

Roode Island or Long Island”. Records exist of nearly 250,000 liters of cider being made that year in Newark.

- By the 18th century, cider became common place in most households in the eastern states of North America

and about one in ten New England families had a cider mill on their property. In 1767, for example, per capita consumption of cider about 40 gallons per year in Massachusetts!

- In 1774, the great American icon of apples and orcharding, John Chapman – more commonly known as Johnny

Appleseed - was born. He introduced apple trees to large parts of eastern USA and became a legend as a result of the symbolic importance he attributed to apples.

- Cider was highly valued by both of the presidents John Adams, who typically had a pint for his breakfast, and

Thomas Jefferson, who grew several varieties of apples at his home in Virginia for making cider.

- The development of cider in the Canadian territories followed a similar pattern to the growth in the USA although,

primarily in Quebec, where the lead was taken by French settlers, not the British. However, the British conquest of 1760 brought in a number of changes. Not the least was Britain’s protectionist trade policy, which set out to ban the local production of cider and instead encouraged beer production by the British industrialists and the import of alcohol, in the form of whisky, gin and rum from the UK and its West Indian colonies.

- In the USA, consumption of cider continued well into the 19th century and was well documented. In 1890, J. M.

Trowbridge published “The Cider Maker’s Hand Book – A Complete Guide for Making and Keeping Pure Cider” and in 1899 it is estimated that over 200 million liters of cider was consumed. However, the immigration of German brewers from Europe helped the local population develop a taste for beer and lager as an alternative to cider and members of the Temperance movement burned and uprooted cider apple orchards. In Canada, small-scale local artisanal production of cider continued, but on an ever decreasing scale.

- By 1915 about 50 million liters was consumed in the USA, considerably less than at the turn of the century. Cider

then became almost non-existent during Prohibition, both in this country and in Canada, when cider became illegal along with other alcoholic beverages.

- After the end of Prohibition, beer consumption and production rapidly increased, whereas cider remained almost

extinct as a drink. Indeed, in Quebec due to a post-Prohibition legislative omission it did not become legally recognised again until 1970.

- In late 1980’s in the USA, the cider revival commenced, but on a relatively small scale, with less than 3 million litres

of cider being consumed in 1990. The Canadian renaissance began at the same time, with the establishment of a cider apple orchard and commercial cidery in British Columbia in 1987 and production of the first Quebec “Ice Cider” in 1989.

- In the late 20th and early 21st centuries, cider consumption and production grew slowly, but steadily. Then, from

about 2010 growth took off. By 2014, 200 million liters of cider was again being consumed in the USA and in British Columbia, cider now taking 3% of the whole market for alcoholic beverages.
