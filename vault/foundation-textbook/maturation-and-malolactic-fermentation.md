# Maturation & Malolactic Fermentation

> Source: Peter Mitchell, *Cider & Perry Production — A Foundation* (2nd ed., 2021), Cider Institute of North America. C. Making Cider & Perry — Stage One: Fruit Selection to Maturation, pages 47–48.


## Maturation



## Overview


In overview, the raw fermented cider will significantly benefit from a period of maturation during which time the following will or may occur:

- Overall, the cider will improve in flavour – it will become less harsh and more ‘rounded’, smooth and complex

in flavour. A key job of the cider maker during the period of maturation - which can last for a few weeks through to several months depending on the type of apple used and the style of cider being made - is to regularly taste the cider until it is judged ready for final processing. A careful check will also be taken to look out for any signs of spoilage, with a range of steps then being taken to prevent problems worsening.

- Malo-lactic fermentation (MLF) may take place. This process is undertaken by lactic acid bacteria, which convert

the malic acid derived from apples to lactic acid and CO2. Lactic acid is only half the strength of malic acid, so the resulting cider will taste less sharp. In addition, the bacteria concerned can also produce a range of other flavoured chemicals that can add to the overall complexity of the product. MLF can occur spontaneously and some producers will actively encourage it through the use of selected strains of bacteria. Depending on the style of cider to be produced and the methods employed by the individual maker concerned, others will choose to prevent MLF. Some further information is included below.

- With cider that is made from cider apples that contain high levels of tannins, a number of natural chemical changes

will occur (along with concomitant changes in color), which will typically result in the cider tasting less bitter and harsh. Additional particulate matter, found in suspension in the freshly fermented and racked raw cider, will also sediment to the bottom of the storage tank and will thus result in the maturing cider becoming fairly clear. During the period of maturation, individual ciders may be blended and, in certain situations, a fining agent such as bentonite or gelatine may be added to improve the clarity or flavour of the cider concerned. Once judged ready by the cider maker, the matured “base” cider will usually go through a range of final downstream processing steps prior to packaging.


## Maturation Management


Key considerations are as follows: 1. As introduced above, bulk cider & perry do benefit from a period of post-fermentation storage (maturation). Although there is no recommended defined length of time for this, then the following should act as initial guidelines: a. For cider made from cider apples, maturation benefit can typically accrue over a 4 to 6 month period to allow time for the apple phenolics to polymerize and thus change in sensory characteristic. b. For cider made from dessert apples and perry from dessert-type pears (not perry pears), one month may be enough time, but this will depend the style of cider required e.g. if the cider maker wants wine-like characteristics to develop, extended maturation periods will be required. c. For perry made from perry pears, then many producers find extended maturation periods are required – possibly up to between 12 – 18 months. 2. During maturation, steps should be taken to ensure NO air contact. The only safe way to achieve this is to keep storage vessels completely FULL. Bag-in-box storage systems are very useful for this purpose, but do ensure that the liner is compatible with cider. Alternatively, blanketing the storage tank head-space with inert gas can help e.g. CO2 (heavier than air, but does dissolve in the cider), nitrogen (is not heavier than air) or argon (expensive, but heavier than air and does not dissolve in the cider). Notwithstanding the above comments, there is some evidence to suggest that, particularly cider made from cider apples, may benefit from controlled micro-oxygenation during storage. 3. Unless you are aiming to encourage a malo-lactic fermentation, then the cider should be stored at a temperature between about 100 to 150 C. 4. Aim to maintain a free SO2 concentration of 30mgl-1 in the cider, unless you are aiming to encourage MLF. 5. Most importantly, the cider should be regularly sampled and monitored during the period of maturation to determine when it is acceptable for downstream processing and packaging for the marketplace and to keep a close check on any off-flavours starting to develop, along with any unwanted changes. In addition to assessing the sensory properties of the cider concerned, analysis for total acidity, volatile acidity, free & total SO2 and possibly diacetyl may be undertaken from time to time. Also look out for any microbial activity (e.g. gas release) that may indicate the cider is possibly suffering from unwanted yeast or bacterial activity.


## Malo-Lactic Fermentation


Malo-lactic fermentation (MLF) is carried out by lactic acid bacteria (LAB) if the conditions are conducive for these bacteria to grow and thrive. LAB are typically always present, so the likelihood of MLF occurring is high unless active steps are taken to inhibit their activity. The main chemical change that occurs is the conversion of malic acid to lactic acid plus CO2. A range of other flavour compounds will also be produced. Although MLF can bring about some positive changes to cider, many strains of the bacteria concerned are also responsible for a number of the potential sensory faults found in cider e.g. excess diacetyl (a sickly ‘butterscotch’ flavour), acetification in perry, “ropiness / oiliness”, excess volatile phenolics (“Barnyard”, medicinal and sweaty leather horse saddle flavours), ethyl catechol (musty), excess biogenic amines and “mousiness”. Very good cider can be made without MLF and, unless the process is very carefully monitored and controlled, including the use of selected strains of LAB, then the risk of spoilage by allowing a spontaneous MLF is too high. The following factors will influence the potential occurrence of MLF:

- Usually occurs towards the end of fermentation, or in store

- More likely to occur when cider left in contact with lees

- Commonly associated with storage of cider in wooden barrels

- Usually only occurs when temperature reaches 15°C and above

- Maintenance of a pH between 3.3 – 3.5 will help suppress the activity of the main LAB strains that can

produce unwanted off-flavours

- SO2 strongly inhibits bacteria – Effective use of SO2, in conjunction with pH and temperature control,

during storage of the cider (i.e. maintain a concentration of at least 30mgl-1 Free SO2) is a key to preventing spontaneous MLF

- High alcohol can inhibit change

- Some yeast strains inhibit MLF


## Non-Microbial Changes During Maturation


A number of chemical changes can also occur to the chemical compounds found in cider during the period of maturation as follows: 1. Phenolics (Tannins)

- Polymerization (primarily of the procyanidins) occurs resulting in a reduction in bitterness. Initially, astringency

can increase due to the production of medium-sized polymers but will then decrease.

- Generally, the concentration will decrease as phenolics bind and precipitate with proteins and cell debris.

- When cider is stored in oak, wood phenolics can be extracted. These can add to the astringent and bitter flavour

of cider, although it should be noted that wood tannins do not seem to ‘marry well’ with cider or perry. However, as oak barrels / vats are reused, extraction decreases. With the decreasing use of oak maturation vats, the use of oak chips may be considered. Although the oxidation effect of wooden vat ageing is lost, wood phenolics are extracted and you will be able to claim that the cider has undergone “oak maturation”! 2. Acid

- In addition to the effect of the MLF on acid levels in cider, organic acids found in cider can also be chemically

changed (esterified - as below) leading to decreased sourness. 3. Esters

- Fruity esters, produced as a result of the yeast metabolism of acids and alcohols, tend to be hydrolyzed back to

the original substrates. Hence, fruitiness will decrease with time. However, chemical esterification of acids with alcohols (e.g. ethyl succinate) will often occur over time, resulting in a more wine-like character being formed in the stored cider.
