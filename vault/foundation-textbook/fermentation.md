# Fermentation

> Source: Peter Mitchell, *Cider & Perry Production — A Foundation* (2nd ed., 2021), Cider Institute of North America. C. Making Cider & Perry — Stage One: Fruit Selection to Maturation, pages 41–46.


## Overview


Although some, mainly small-scale, cider makers will carry out fermentation using ‘wild’ indigenous yeasts, the majority of producers will add selected strains of yeast (Saccharomyces species) to help ensure that they can always achieve a consistent, clean and good flavoured product free from spoilage. Although some very good ciders can be produced through the activity of ‘wild’ yeasts, results are not predictable. Yeast nutrients are also commonly added, a practice that also goes back for many hundred years when it was not uncommon in centuries past for a cider maker add a leg of mutton to “help fermentation”. The story about adding a dead rat, however, is a myth and these days, specially formulated yeast nutrients are available. Fermentation will typically take 2 to 4 weeks (sometimes longer, sometimes shorter) during which time the yeast will convert the sugar found in apple juice to alcohol (ethanol) and carbon dioxide (CO2); the latter being vented out through an airlock in the vat. The yeast will also produce other flavour compounds during fermentation – and their production will vary according to the strain of yeast used, one of the main causes of the different flavors found in different ciders. At the end of fermentation, unless an ‘arrested fermentation’ has been carried out to produce a ‘naturally sweet’ cider (not an easy practice and not commonly carried out), all the sugar will have been metabolised and the SG, when measured, will be at 1.000 or slightly below (alcohol has a lower SG than water). At this time the ‘raw’ cider will be carefully “racked” off (separated from) the “lees” (spent yeast cells and other debris found at the bottom of the fermentation vat) into a storage vessel for maturation. ## Preparation of the Juice for Fermentation


Two main aspects should be considered and appropriate analysis undertaken: Potential alcohol production and pH control


## Potential Alcohol Production


During fermentation, sugar is metabolized by the yeast to form roughly equal proportions of ethanol and CO2 The measurement of Specific Gravity (e.g. using a hydrometer) provides a reasonable guide to the amount of sugar present, hence the potential alcohol concentration (%ABV) can be estimated as follows: Step 1: Estimate the likely drop in Gravity during fermentation. For the purposes of this rough calculation, always presume that the juice will stop fermenting at a Specific Gravity (SG) of 1.000 - e.g. if the initial SG is 1.050, then the likely drop in Gravity will be 50 degrees (500). Step 2: % ABV = Gravity drop ÷ 7.5 e.g. 50 = 6.7% ABV 7.5 Alternatively, a cider maker may well wish to target a specified alcohol concentration in the fermented cider and calculate the amount of extra sugar to be added to achieve this, a process called “Chapitalisation”. The necessary calculations are as follows: Step 1: Calculate overall required Gravity change during fermentation: Overall required Gravity = %ABV X 7.5 Example: 10% ABV x 7.5 = 750 Step 2: Calculate the extra Gravity required over & above of what is already present in the juice: Extra Gravity = Required minus already present Example: 750 minus 500 = 250 Step 3: Calculate the amount of sugar (in grams) required as follows: Amount sugar (g) = Fermentation volume (ltrs) x Gravity x 2.6. Example: Sugar = 100 ltrs x 250 x 2.6 = 6,500g


## pH Control


Prior to fermentation, the juice should be analysed for its acid concentration and – SEPARATELY – its pH. Although acid concentration and pH are interrelated, two separate data points should be determined. Each parameter provides different information: Simply, the measurement of acid concentration provides information about the actual amount of acid present (i.e. grams per litre concentration), whereas the pH provides an indication of the strength of the acid. By definition an acid is a substance that liberates hydrogen ions when in solution: e.g. Hydrochloric acid: HCl H+ + Cl- The concentration of an acid can be directly measured by titration (mixing) of an acid with an alkaline to produce a solution that is neutral (this point is determined), whereas pH is a measurement (using a pH meter or indicator strips) of how many hydrogen ions (H+) are liberated in a solution and thus is an indication of the strength of the acid present. Generally, but NOT always, the greater the acid concentration the more H+ ions are liberated. pH is measured on a scale of 1 to 14; with pH 7.0 being neutral. The pH scale is inverse, so the more H + ions that are present, the lower is the reported pH value below pH 7.0. Often in apple juice and cider there is NOT a direct relationship between the amount of acid present and the amount of H+ ions released. This will depend on: o An individual acid’s nature o The variety of acids present in any given solution o The amount and variety of other substances present In high acid varieties of apple, most of the acid is malic acid and there is a close relationship between acid and the pH. But in low acid varieties, the mixture of acids is more complex and the relationship between total acid concentration and pH is less clearly defined i.e. juices with the same total acid concentration can show different pH’s and vice versa. The ideal pH level in juice prior to fermentation should be between pH 3.3 - 3.5 (NO higher than pH 3.8). The best way to achieve this is by blending together different varieties of fruit, but this is not always possible. Alternatively, food grade malic acid can be added to decrease the pH or calcium carbonate (chalk) can be used to increase the pH. Unfortunately, it is practically impossible to calculate the amount of acid or chalk to add to the juice to predictably alter the pH by a specified amount. Therefore, you should aim to adjust the acid concentration towards an overall concentration (including the concentration of the existing acid in the original juice) of 5.0 - 6.0 gl-1 (measured as malic acid), then recheck the pH. Small scale trials should be used to determine the dose of malic acid or chalk to be used, before adding the overall amount to the total volume of juice that requires treatment.


## Yeast Selection, Use and Management


The most widely used form of yeast culture used is the so-called “Active Dried Yeast” (ADY). A wide range of strains are available – most originally isolated from successful ‘wild yeast’ fermentations - in a variety of pack sizes to suit all scales of production. ADY is usually added in conjunction with sulphiting and will often be necessary for fermentation to proceed successfully. However, producers can also add a culture without the use of SO2; aiming to out-compete any other microorganisms. As a general guide, many producers find the so-called “Champagne” or white-wine yeasts strains the most successful ones for cider production, either used as a single strain or, occasionally, combining two or more strains together. Typical dosage rates for ADY are 20 – 30g per hectoliter of juice. ADY will need careful re-hydration before it is added to the juice for fermentation, either (and preferably) using special yeast rehydration nutrients (follow manufacturer’s instructions), which are designed to supply the yeast with the necessary micronutrients (e.g. vitamins), or just by using water as follows:

1. Use potable tap water at 40°C (no higher than 43°C) 2. Gradually stir in the yeast – 10% w/v suspension e.g. 10g per 100ml. Add yeast TO water, not vice versa 3. Stopper or cover container and leave for 20 minutes. Do not mix. 4. Before adding the yeast suspension to juice, aerate the yeast and also make sure that the temperature difference between the suspension & the juice is no more than 5°C. Thus the suspension will need gradual cooling, either by running cold water over the outside of the contained used, or gradually adding the colder juice directly to the yeast suspension over about a 10 minute period. The maximum period of time from when the yeast is first added to the water and the suspension added to the juice should be no more than 30 minutes. Yeasts require the following nutrients for growth & vitality and to complete a successful fermentation: 1. An energy source and carbon skeletons, which are obtained from sugar. Ethanol and CO2 are produced as metabolic by-products. If Chapitalisation is to be carried out, additional sugar ideally should be ‘batch-fed’ to the yeast during fermentation. As a guide, at every 100 gravity drop during fermentation, add up to 30gl -1 of sugar at any one time. This timing will often link to the timings for adding other yeast nutrients. Ensure the sugar is completely dissolved before adding to the “must”. 2. A source of nitrogen for the production of proteins, known as Yeast Assimilable Nitrogen (YAN). Yeasts are able to utilize amino-acids (Free Amino Nitrogen – FAN) and also inorganic nitrogen (e.g. ammonium – NH4+). Apple juice contains amino-acids but, essentially, no inorganic nitrogen. The concentration of nitrogen found in juice is often not in sufficient quantities to meet the yeast’s requirements, therefore an additional source of nitrogen will normally be required, typically an extra 75 - 200mgl-1 N. The amount to add will depend on the initial level in the apple juice itself, the yeast strain used and the extent of fermentation. Symptoms of nitrogen deficiency include the production of hydrogen sulfide (H2S – a ‘bad-egg smell’) and/or a stuck or sluggish fermentation. Additional nitrogen will be supplied if using a proprietary yeast nutrient and also by using diammonium phosphate (DAP), which contains 21% w/w available nitrogen. Trials will be required to establish ideal nutrient regimes for different scenarios. 3. Vitamins & minerals also play a crucial role in yeast growth & activity. The two main vitamins that can cause problems by their deficiency in cider fermentation are: a) Thiamine (B1). This is also degraded by SO2. This vitamin can be added directly to the juice (< + 0.5mgl-1) or can be added as part of a proprietary yeast nutrient. b) Pantothenate (B5). Deficiency is also associated with H2S formation. About 0.25mgl-1 is typically required; either added directly or as part of a proprietary yeast nutrient. 4. Oxygen is also crucial to ensure yeast growth in the early stages of fermentation and also to ensure the resulting yeasts cells are healthy. In practice, an effort should be taken to ensure that the fresh juice is thoroughly aerated prior to fermentation. The following points should also be noted:

- Seek manufacturer’s advice and instructions when using a proprietary blended yeast nutrient.

- As a guide, half of the proprietary yeast nutrient complex should be added to the juice about 6 - 12 hours after

yeast addition and the other half about 1/3rd of the way through fermentation.

- DAP is toxic to yeasts whilst being re-hydrated and also, ideally, should not be added in the very early stage of

fermentation. If DAP is being used, then add ‘little and often’, but aim to ensure that the whole dose is added in during the first half of fermentation.

A basic guide for managing fermentation is as follows: 1. Ensure the vessel is made from food-grade material and is effectively sanitized before use. 2. Use an air lock. Useful as a quick visual check to see that fermentation is progressing satisfactorily and will keep debris (and air, in the later stages of fermentation) out of the vessel, but allow CO2 to escape. 3. Maintain an appropriate temperature in the fermentation vessel, matched to the individual yeast strain(s) being used. The normal temperature range for most fermentation yeasts is between 12°C - 23°C. 4. Regularly (at least daily) monitor fermentation progress: S.G.and temperature. It is also important keep a check on any production of H2S as this is often indicative of nutrient deficiency and other “stress” factors. 5. Undertake yeast nutrition, as outlined above. A number of factors can affect the rate of fermentation and the following list can also be used as a trouble-shooting guide in case problems emerge (e.g. slow or stuck fermentations): 1) Yeast numbers and strain. If an ADY has not been added or incorrectly handled, it may well be that there are not sufficient numbers of viable yeast cells present. 2) Temperature. In practice this is often the main reason why fermentation can be sluggish or “stuck”. Ideally, if the juice is warm enough (150 – 200 C dependant on yeast strain) before the yeast is added, then as fermentation is exothermic, often sufficient heat will be generated to keep the yeast active. It should be noted, however, in large fermentation tanks excess heat can build-up, therefore chilling may well be needed. 3) Yeast nutrient deficiency. A lack of nutrients can lead to a sluggish or stuck fermentation, even if no H2S is generated at the same time. 4) Oxygen. Although it is essential to ensure anaerobic conditions during most of fermentation, yeasts do require oxygen to grow up to form a healthy population during the early stages of fermentation. ## Fermentation End & Racking


Fermentation typically ends when all the sugars have been metabolized (“dryness”). In cider, the S.G. will fall to 1.000 or, in the case of relatively high alcohol ciders, below 1.000. There will be no further changes to S.G. It should be noted, that in perry, due to the likelihood of it containing un-fermentable sugar (sorbitol), fermentation may finish at a S.G. above 1.000 Yeast and other solid material will flocculate and settle to the bottom of the vessel to form the lees, leaving the raw cider relatively clear. One criterion that should be used when selecting a yeast is to choose one that will flocculate well. Poorly flocculating strains will leave a cloudy cider, which may be difficult to stabilize. Low temperature will stimulate the flocculation process. After the end of fermentation it is important to rack the clear raw cider off the lees. Usually this should be done as soon as possible so as to minimize the effects of yeast autolysis (notably the formation of unpleasant smelling organic sulphur compounds from these ‘rotting’ yeast cells). The aim should be to rack the cider within a maximum of 2 weeks after the end of fermentation and much earlier if possible. Racking at the end of fermentation will also produce a more stable product; less likely to become infected by unwanted micro-organisms. Key objectives at racking are to:

- Minimize waste and maximize cider recovery.

- Avoid aeration as far as possible and retain dissolved CO2. Oxygen pick-up at this stage can lead to excessive

oxidation of the product and may also lead to microbial spoilage of the cider (e.g. acetification) in storage.

- Ensure safety of personnel – remember that CO2 gas can lead to asphyxiation.

- Do not collapse the vessel when emptying it! Remember, when you draw-off the cider, the volume in the tank

that it took up will need to be replaced by something else; so open up a tank vent.

- Assure good standards of hygiene – Ensure the reception vessel and transfer pipes have been effectively

sanitised.

- Carry the process out efficiently & effectively

At the time of racking, the cider will often be sampled and analysed for SG, total & free SO2, total acidity and pH. The pH will be re-adjusted if necessary and usually the cider is also re-sulfited to give ca. 30mg l-1 free SO2. The cider may also be:

- Fined

- Centrifuged

- Coarse filtered

- Blended

- Diluted, sweetened and re-fermented if the cider has a significant unbalanced flavour

After about 4 days, a representative sample of the recently racked cider should undergo a full analytical assessment for: Alcohol content, total acidity, pH, SG and free & total SO2. A sensory analysis should also be undertaken.
