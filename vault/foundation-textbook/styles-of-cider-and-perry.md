# Styles of Cider and Perry

> Source: Peter Mitchell, *Cider & Perry Production — A Foundation* (2nd ed., 2021), Cider Institute of North America. F. Cider and Perry — An Appreciation, pages 61–61.

Cider and Perry - An Appreciation F. CIDER AND PERRY – AN APPRECIATION

Cider and perry are wonderfully versatile products – probably more so than any other single category of alcoholic beverage – and are thus suitable for many different occasions. Products can be: q Still or sparkling. q Bone dry through to sweet. q “Gin-bright” or naturally cloudy. q Found in a range of colors from very pale straw (almost white) through to rich, deep gold. q Available in a range of alcoholic strengths. q Found as a draught keg product sold in a pub, in cans or PET bottles and in a range of glass bottles of different styles and sizes. q …. and last, but not least, having a huge range of flavours that reflect the variety of apples and pears used and the different processes employed by the individual cider or perry maker concerned. In the main traditional and established cider producing countries of the world, there are some key national and regional differences in the products available, notably: q French ciders are typically low in alcohol, often fairly sweet, commonly paler than English “Bittersweet” ciders, can have some “sherry-like” qualities and are frequently found as ‘naturally sparkling’ products (i.e. have had a secondary in-bottle fermentation). “Cidre Doux” is usually any cider up to 3% in strength, “Demi-Sec” is between 3 to 5% and “Cidre Brut” is a strong cider of 5% alcohol and above, whilst “Cidre Bouche” is a sweet cider that has undergone a secondary fermentation in the bottle. q Spanish ciders are usually quite dry, are typically non-carbonated (still) and often (purposely) have a noticeable acetic acid flavor. q Ciders from the eastern counties of England have traditionally been made from dessert apples and thus display fresh, acidic and ‘high’ fruity notes typical of the type of apples concerned. q Most, but not all ciders from west of England have the distinctive taste (bitterness), mouth-feel (astringency and ‘body’) and flavours (‘Low’, ‘heavy’, ‘thick’ fruity notes and spicy, woody and smoky characteristics) of traditional high-tannin cider apples. Ciders from Devon can often have much more woody flavours than their counterparts from the other western counties, are typically rich mahogany-colored and are commonly fairly acidic. Traditional Somerset ciders are often quite dry and typically display the fruity and ‘tannin-like’ qualities of the local bittersweet and bittersharp apples used. Products from Herefordshire and other West Midland counties, as well as having a balanced combination of bitterness, astringency, body and acidity, will often be fairly fruity in flavor; q Perry – predominantly made in the West Midlands, UK and also Austria and France – as well as having usually a characteristic fresh pear and pear-drop aroma, typically has a very light, subtle and complex flavor that may include tropical, summer and citrus fruits. In the new growth markets of the world, notably in North America , much of the drive for the current growth in cider comes from the craft revolution that has been occurring in the beer sector. This striving for authenticity and provenance is extremely well suited to cider & perry production. Currently, there is also much product innovation occurring as the young cider industry explores the versatility that cider can provide, although maybe the iconic apple and pear by on their own should not be completely forgotten! Possibly, if it wasn’t for cider just made from apples alone, the US constitution may never have been written!
