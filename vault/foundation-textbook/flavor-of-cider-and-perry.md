Cider and Perry - An Appreciation

Although the term “tasting” is commonly used to describe what we do when we evaluate food and drink - whether it be on a formal or informal basis – the use of this word is really a misnomer as it, in reality, underrates and misrepresents the broader range of senses we actually use. “Organoleptic analysis” (the scientific name given) or the phrase “sensory appreciation” better represent the wide range of senses (including sight, smell, taste, mouth-feel and even hearing and touch) that we use when appraising products such as cider and perry. It should not be forgotten that when assessing a product, the packaging it is in often has a significant effect on our perception of it. Other acids include: Quinic, citric, citramalic, succinic, lactic. Cider and Perry - An Appreciation Cider procyanidins exist as a chemical chain of linked procyanidin units and are both bitter and astringent – the shorter chains (2 to 4 units long) are bitter (commonly referred to as “hard tannin” and properly called “oligomers”), whereas the larger chains (5 to 7 units long) are more astringent (“soft tannin” or, properly, “polymers”). There are a number of aspects that affect the overall concentration of phenolics and the ratio of oligomers to polymers e.g. the apple variety. During post-fermentation maturation of cider, polymerization (chemical linkage of individual units) of phenolics occurs resulting in a reduction in bitterness. Initially, astringency can increase due to the production of medium-sized polymers but will then decrease as the large phenolic chains literally fall-out of solution in the cider due to their size and chemical binding with other material found in the cider. When cider is stored in ‘new’ oak, wood phenolics can be extracted. These can add to the overall flavor of cider and perry, but the result is not always positive as wood tannins do not seem to ‘marry’ well with most ciders or perries – this is different from wine, where the ‘mix’ between grape and wood tannin is often desirable in the finished product. However, as oak barrels and vats are reused, extraction of wood phenolics decreases and some ciders can benefit from storage in ‘old’ wood due to, for example, micro-oxidation. Freshly used spirit barrels – especially rum barrels (much sought after by many smaller producers) – are a different matter however, as the flavours of the two beverages often do seem to go particularly well together! Aroma Compounds One of the main attractions of cider and perry is due to a wide variety of volatile compounds present in the drinks at fairly low concentrations. These also distinguish one specific product from another. The flavour of cider and perry is significantly attributable to these so-called “Aroma Compounds”, in addition to their sugar, phenolic (tannin) and acid content. Since the advent of modern laboratory analytical techniques, hundreds of these compounds have been identified, which can be grouped as follows: 1. Fusel Alcohols e.g. phenethyl alcohol (a rose-like aroma); 2. Esters e.g. amyl acetate (pear drops); 3. Volatile phenolics e.g. vinyl phenol (spicy) 4. Aldehydes e.g. acetaldehyde (bruised apples); 5. Ketones e.g. diacetyl (butter-scotch) 6. Dioxanes – a range of similar chemical compounds that have a unique and distinctly “cidery” aroma, thus may well be responsible for sensory differentiation of cider and perry from other alcoholic beverages. These aroma compounds are mainly formed by microbial activity occurring during fermentation and maturation from the precursors found in the original fruit. In addition there are other natural chemical changes that take place throughout the whole of the cider and perry making process. The final types and concentrations of the aroma compounds will be a main factor that causes one particular product to have a different flavour from the next. Finally, it is important to note that all chemical compounds that can be found, including many that result in the formation of off-flavors, can have a positive effect on the sensory properties of cider and perry. However, they will all have a limiting concentration at which the positive effect turns into a negative one. For example, diacetyl at levels 2 - 4mgl -1 is accepted as being important in rounding-off the flavor of wines and in cider, however, diacetyl is possibly only acceptable up to 0.6mgl-1. The actual level will depend on the specific type of cider concerned.
