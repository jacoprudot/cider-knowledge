## Cider Apple & Perry Pear Production in Europe


Historically, the Cider Industry is inextricably linked with the production of apples and pears. Currently, as the largest producer of cider in the world, UK makers use nearly 60% of all apples grown in the UK and, since the beginning of the 21st century, have been steadily reducing their use of imported juice and concentrate and increasing their commitment to UK grown fruit. This needs to be viewed in the context of an overall shrinking apple and pear production industry in the UK – reduced from over 22,500 hectares in 1994/95 to 16,000 hectares in 2004/05 (DEFRA, 2005). The planted area of cider apples and perry pears, however, has increased from just over 3,400 hectares in 1995 to about 7,000 hectares in 2014 and continues to grow. The total value of cider apples and perry pears marketed in 2004 was just over £9.6 million (DEFRA, 2005), but in 2015 the 250,000 tonnes produced is currently estimated to be valued at nearly £30 million. The traditional locations for the UK’s cider industry – in the Channel Islands, the eastern counties of England, the West Midlands, Gloucestershire and the Welsh borders and down through Somerset, Devon and Cornwall – is a result of these regions’ suitability for growing apples and pears. Main areas for growing perry pears are generally confined to Gloucestershire, Herefordshire, Monmouthshire and Worcestershire although in the mid 20th century significant acreage was established in Somerset to meet the requirements of the Showering’s factory at Shepton Mallet. Most cider fruit was historically grown in the West Country simply because it was more profitable in the south-east to grow and sell dessert and culinary fruit direct to London and its fruit and vegetable markets, leaving the more distant western counties to produce a durable product (cider or perry) that would survive transportation. However, while the juice of any apple can be used to make cider (south-eastern counties of England have traditionally used cull (reject) dessert or culinary fruit), cider apples and perry pears were originally selected and are particularly valued by many producers for this purpose. France, however, is currently the single largest producer of cider apples in the world, with some 8,700 hectares under plantation and producing 260,000 tonnes of fruit per year (AICV , 2014). Much of this fruit is processed into cider apple (“Bittersweet”) concentrate and is sold on the world market. Due to current world demand for Bittersweet concentrate, notably by the USA, existing cider apple orchards are being expanded and new ones are also being planted in France and other European countries.


## Cider Apple & Perry Pear Production in North America


In North America and as described above, from the early 17th century, many cider apple orchards were planted in the Eastern States of the USA. From the late 19th century and throughout Prohibition, however, most of these orchards were destroyed and some of the original varieties of cider apples became almost extinct on American soil and have not been cultivated on any significant scale subsequently. Since about 2010, however, there has been a resurgence of interest in growing cider apples and work is being undertaken to identify and select varieties that will be suitable for use. In 1905 the first comprehensive compendium of varieties of known apple trees planted in the USA was published by the US Department of Agriculture (Nomenclature of the Apple: A Catalogue of the Known Varieties Referred to in American Publications from 1804 to 1904). Around 14,000 different varieties were listed. Many of these would have been used for making cider, however a few varieties were highly prized and thus identified for such use. ## Cider Apples and Perry Pears


Although as outlined below almost any type of apple and many varieties of pears can and are successfully used to make cider or perry, there are certain fruit varieties that are typically characterised (and valued as such) as being suitable for making these fermented products. These so-called “Cider Apples” & “Perry Pears” possess several or all of the following characteristics:

- A ‘chewy’ flesh to facilitate juice extraction;

- A relatively high sugar content for alcohol production;

- Tannins (more properly called Phenolics) that provide the characteristic taste and flavour of ‘traditional’

cider and perry;

- A pleasing apple / pear taste and aroma in the fermented products.

Classification of Cider Apples The original and most widely used classification of cider apples was developed by the researchers at the Long Ashton Research Station (LARS) in the UK in 1903 and is as follows: Acid Content (gl-1) Tannin Content (gl-1) Sharps ~ high in acid, low in tannin > 4.5 < 2.0 Sweets ~ low in acid and tannin < 4.5 < 2.0 Bittersharps ~ high in acid and tannin > 4.5 > 2.0 Bittersweets ~ low in acid and high in tannin < 4.5 > 2.0 In the UK, the selection and introduction of varieties specifically for cider became established in the 17th and 18th centuries and was heralded by the widespread planting of Foxwhelp and Scudamore’s Crab, both renowned for the quality of their cider, but harsh and unpleasant for eating. Local preferences developed and, by the 19 th century, Devon favored sweet varieties, Somerset bittersweets, Gloucestershire sharps and Herefordshire & Worcestershire a mixture of sharps and bittersweets, resulting in distinctive regional ciders. The selection of high quality cider varieties still continues today in response to the need for high productivity and to meet changes in the public’s perception of a quality cider. Choice of Varieties The choice of the cider or perry variety (“cultivar”) depends on two primary factors: 1. Properties required by the cider or perry maker – notably juice qualities (e.g. level of tannin), and time of supply i.e. early (September), mid (October - November) or late (November – December) season; 2. Suitability of the cultivar to the specific growing conditions of the locality concerned, how the grower wishes to organise and spread the orcharding load and which varieties may attract a premium price. Growers will also aim to ensure that cross-pollination will occur between varieties although some cultivars (e.g. Vilberie) are self- pollinating. A traditional cider orchard contained a wide range of varieties, partially reflecting the importance given to producing ciders from a blend of different apples but also as a means of spreading out the period of cider making throughout the

autumn. It was also recognised that a mixture of varieties is required to produce a well-balanced cider. During the latter part of the 20th century, however, fewer individual varieties were planted as g rowers were keen only to plant those that would reliably crop every year (e.g. Michelin and Dabinett), were disease resistant and were easy and cost effective to manage. More recently, a new generation of 21 st century varieties (mild bittersweet & bittersharp) was bred to achieve regular and early cropping. ## Cider Apple Orcharding


Up until about 1970 in European countries, cider apples were mainly produced from orchards based on the use of the so-called “standard” tree, which have a long trunk and will grow to 6 metres or more in height. Most trees are planted about 10 metres apart giving a density of about 100 trees per hectare (40 per acre) and a modest yield of between 15 to 25 tonnes per hectare (6 – 10 tonnes per acre). Orchards would have a dual use, as they would have allowed farm animals to graze beneath the trees. During the 1950’s experimental work was undertaken at the Long Ashton Research Station in the UK to develop bush orchards that would result in improved yields. Stimulated by Incentive Planting Schemes implemented by Bulmers and the Taunton Cider Company, the first generation of bush orchards were established in the early 1970’s. In a period of 5 years, over 400 hectares (1000 acres) of intensive commercial cider orchards were planted. These so called “bush apple trees”, o are planted at a density of up to 750 trees per hectare (300 per acre) in rows about 5.5 meters apart (to allow for machinery access) and 2 to 3 meters between trees in each row depending on variety; o have a short trunk and will typically only grow up to about 6 meters maximum; o commonly will yield 35 to 50 tonnes per hectare (15 to 20 tons per acre) of orchard. Some of the original first generation of bush trees continue to surpass expectations even now in the early 21 st century, although a second generation of orchards was established in the 1990’s in response to growth of the UK Cider Industry at that time. More recently and as indicated above, a third generation of cultivars were developed in response to the lack of suitable early maturing varieties. Crosses were made between Dabinett & Michelin and the early juice & dessert varieties of James Grieve & Worcester Pearmain. A selection of 29 new cultivars resulted – known as the “Girls” as they were primarily given female names – suitable for planting under different conditions. The growing of cider apple trees involves the following key aspects: The use of rootstocks onto which a scion (a short length of stem, taken from the cider variety of choice) is budded. Cider apple varieties grown on their own roots are generally too vigorous, thus a rootstock is selected that will control the growth of trees to within a predetermined size and improve cropping. The original ancestors of modern rootstocks are the wild apple species M. sylvestris and M. pumila. The specific rootstock chosen is determined by the scion cultivar, the orchard site and intended tree planting density. The main rootstocks currently used are:

- MM106 Most commonly used rootstock. Will form semi-dwarfing trees between 4 – 6 meters high.

- M26 Smaller than MM106 and not suitable for weak varieties. Less vigor but more precocious

cropping.

- MM111 Vigorous, producing a larger tree than MM106. Tolerant of drought and wind. Useful for

weaker varieties e.g. Dabinett.

- M25 The strongest rootstock. Produces the largest, most robust trees for difficult sites. Used for

standard trees. Not suitable for vigorous varieties such as Ellis Bitter.

- M9 Although a dwarf rootstock, typically used in intensive trellis-based orchard systems, it is

generally not suitable when machine harvesting is undertaken as the roots tend to be brittle.

- Bud 9 A cold-hardy dwarfing rootstock. Needs supporting and not suitable for machine harvesting due

to its brittle roots. Collection of Perry Pears

New rootstocks continue to be bred and released. For example, since the early 1970’s the Geneva® apple rootstock breeding program has been operating, which aims to maximize efficient production of high quality apples in commercial orchards. Specific objectives have included increased resistance to diseases such Fire Blight & Wooly Apple Aphids and the modification of tree branching and root architecture. These programmes continue to result in the release of new rootstocks in the USA and other countries of the world. One particular challenge involved in growing cider apples and perry pears, when compared with dessert and culinary fruit, is that many varieties are strongly biennial in their cropping. Indeed, an overall pattern of “on” or “off” years across a whole region may become established for several years at a time. In a new orchard, adequately preparing the site typically includes:

- Installing an effective soil drainage system.

- Adjusting the fertility through the use of fertilizers and lime before

planting to provide the ideal nutrient status for young trees.

- Fencing the whole orchard to keep wild and farm animals away

and providing tree guards to protect the young tree from damage by rabbits.

- Establishing windbreaks to reduce damage to tree roots and

anchorage, encourage shoot growth and provide shelter for pollinating insects e.g. bees.

- Putting up stakes to which the young growing tree will be tied.

Regular husbandry in the orchard includes:

- Pruning to produce and maintain a “center-leader” tree shape, forming an

“A ” shaped hedgerow canopy along the rows. This allows the best sun-light penetration into the tree to ensure a good source of energy for growth, flower and fruit production. Pruning also helps renew old wood for young growth, maintains a good tree shape for machinery access, improves air movement through the tree canopy to suppress diseases, improves spray penetration to all parts of the tree and removes dead and diseased branches.

- Maintaining the ground and grass around the trees. For example, many

orchards will keep a “herbicide-strip” (an area clear of grass) under the trees to reduce the competition for nutrients. Machinery wheel-ruts will be smoothed out and grass will be mowed prior to harvesting.

- Maintenance of an appropriate soil pH (optimum pH 6.5 for apple trees)

and nutrient status – notably levels of nitrogen, phosphate, potash and magnesium need to be maintained at an appropriate level.

- Tree and fruit disease and pest control. Examples of the main pests and

fungal diseases of cider apples and trees are: o Apple sawfly – the insect may cause substantial fruit drop shortly after blossom resulting in serious crop loss; o Rosy-apple aphid – this pest spreads rapidly to cause severe crop loss through a reduction in fruit size; o Apple scab (a fungus) – the most economically important disease of cider apples and can significantly affect orchard productivity; o Blossom wilt – another potentially serious fungal disease of cider apples which causes flowers to wilt. Some varieties are especially susceptible, such as Somerset Redstreak. There are two main ways used to control pests - through the use of chemical pesticides and, increasingly over recent years, encouraging the numbers of beneficial predatory insects in the orchard that will, in turn, reduce many potential pest species to insignificant levels. Integrated Crop Management (ICM) systems are under continual development and much work has been led and funded by the UK Cider Industry. These involve encouraging beneficial insects to thrive which, when coupled with regular monitoring for pests and the careful selection and use of pesticides, reduces the amount of chemical spray that needs to be used. Similarly, with fungal diseases, fungicidal spays are used, but usually only when disease thresholds (incidence of disease in the orchard) are exceeded and weather forecasts indicate that conditions may encourage the outbreak of fungal infection and growth. The list of approved pesticides and fungicides is under constant review, most chemicals include a minimum harvest interval (the time that must be left between the application of the spray and the harvesting of the fruit) and all operators must be trained and certificated in the handling and use of such pest and disease control products. There are also a number of orchards that provide fruit to some cider producers for use in making organic cider. There is a limited and strictly controlled list of chemicals that are certified for use in the production of apples for organic use. Fruit Harvesting and Supply to the Cider Mill As further detailed below, it is important to harvest apples at the correct stage of ripeness. If apples are unripe: o The sugar content will be too low. o The flavour will not be fully developed. Use of over-ripe fruit, that has been left on the orchard ‘floor’ for too long, should also be avoided as off-flavors can be produced in the cider and, of greater economic significance, apples will start to rot due to contamination with the fungus Phytophthora which is present in the soil and is prevalent, particularly during a wet autumn. Brown rot, another fungal infection that can start whilst the fruit is still on the tree, is also a major cause of fruit rotting. The use of rotten fruit for making cider should be completely avoided as it: o Causes problems when the fruit is milled and pressed, which results in a reduced juice yield. o Is a major source of microbial infection which, in turn, will be the cause of off-flavours in the cider and other fermentation problems. o Renders sulphur dioxide ineffective. Over recent years, cider makers have become increasingly strict with respect to the quality of fruit they will accept at the mill and it is not unknown for loads of apples to be rejected if they have not met the standard required. Many producers have a Code of Practice for apple supply and some operate incentive schemes that encourage fruit growers to only supply the highest quality apples. Significant work continues to be undertaken to improve the quality of fruit grown and supplied for cider production. It should be noted that harvested fruit is a food product and its transportation from the orchard to the cider mill is normally covered by legislation in most countries of the world. Traditionally, cider fruit was hand-harvested from the orchard ground after a natural fall following ripening on the tree. Long wooden poles would sometimes be used to remove apples higher up in the tree. Over the years, as casual labor to do this work became more scarce and expensive and, with the widespread introduction of the large intensive orchards in the 1970’s, mechanical harvesting methods were developed and introduced. It is, however, recognized, that in some States and Provinces of Canada that resistance may be encountered with the authorities in respect to using ground-fallen fruit in cider production, even though current evidence shows that bacterial pathogens (e.g. E. coli) are destroyed in the early stages of the fermentation process. The basis of the mechanical harvesting system is the harvester or pick-up machine. There are three main types in current use: 1. Small pedestrian-steered self-propelled machines. Although labor intensive, these are suitable for the smaller and standard orchards. 2. Tractor–mounted machines. 3. Self-propelled integral machines. Although relatively expensive, they do have a low requirement for labor. Alongside these harvesters, which pick up fruit from the ground, apple growers often also use mechanical tree shakers and equipment that sweeps or blows fallen apples from beneath the tree into the middle of the alleyway for easy pickup.

In the late 20th century, development work commenced to start growing cider apple trees along vine-style trellises. This system is designed to allow a second generation of harvesting machines collect apples directly from the tree and thus avoid them falling to the orchard floor. The key advantages of this system include: o Avoidance of apple bruising which, although not a problem for cider makers per se, can lead to early fruit rot setting in. o Reduced labor costs. Perry Pear Orcharding Unlike cider apples where considerable investment in new orchards and development of new management techniques was undertaken in the second half of the 20 th century in direct response to demand for the products, perry orcharding has not received anywhere near the same degree of emphasis. Most perry pear orchards can now only be found in the Western region of the UK and in defined areas of France and Austria and are often small and, not uncommonly, include trees that are hundreds of years old. Fortunately, perry pear trees are particularly long lived, which has helped to ensure the continual production of fine perries for the current-day consumer. Renovation of old orchards is being undertaken and, during recent years, some new planting has occurred. In recognition of the decline of perry orchards over the past 100 years or so a number of recent initiatives have now been implemented. These include the establishment of the National Collection of Perry Pears at Malvern and at Hartpury; both of which are managed by the Hartpury Trust and the international Slow Food Association, which founded the Three Counties Perry Presidium. In North America, the Agricultural Research Service (ARS) in association with Oregon State University manage a National Clonal Germplasm Repository of perry pears in Corvallis, Oregon. The planting, establishment and management of a perry pear orchard involves the same basic principles and practices as those for a cider apple orchard, however there are some differences. Although there was development of a few bush perry orchards on seedling or quince rootstocks in the mid 20 th century, this practice never became properly established and most pear trees found in the small number of existing perry orchards are large. Indeed, one of the challenges of growing perry trees are that they are very slow growing and the resulting mature tree is typically large – 15 meters or so high – which also makes the trees difficult to manage and harvest. One disease of particular concern to perry pear growers is Fireblight, caused by the bacterium erwinia amylovora. Unfortunately, there are no recommended chemical control measures and the disease has caused severe devastation in some orchards. Work has been carried out to identify and propagate pear rootstocks resistant to the disease – for example the “OHxF Series” developed in the USA in association with Oregon State University. Overall, perry pear production currently remains a small-scale industry, with the fruit typically being produced in conjunction with a grass as a duel cropping system on a multi-product farm or from old, long-established traditional orchards. The orcharding is not on the same scale and does not involve the same approach as the large, intensive and specialist commercial cider apple orchards. However, this may change if – supported by industry promotional campaigns – the consumer again develops a real taste for the fine product of perry.
