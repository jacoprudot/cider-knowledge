# Faults in Cider & Perry — A Summary

> Source: Peter Mitchell, *Cider & Perry Production — A Foundation* (2nd ed., 2021), Cider Institute of North America. F. Cider and Perry — An Appreciation, pages 66–69.

Cider and Perry - An Appreciation Faults in Cider & Perry – A Summary Unfortunately a number of faults can occur and do exist in cider and perry. It is important that all those involved in the production, marketing, sales and promotion of the products are able to recognise these potential problems. Those involved in the manufacture of the beverages concerned, in particular, should know, understand and implement strategies and methods that can prevent faults from arising in the first place. Fortunately, cider and perry are very safe beverages and any of the main faults that can occur are typically not associated with human health problems - but they will detract from the enjoyment of consumption and can make the product concerned, undrinkable. Outline details of potential organoleptic (sensory) faults of cider and perry are shown in Table 5. Excess SO2 – “burnt match, striking match, sulfur dioxide, choking” 2. Sulfidic - “Rotten egg, H2S, reduced sulfur, drains, rotting yeast, burnt rubber, shrimp-like, cooked vegetable, cooked cabbage” Faults usually caused by: Excess addition/concentration of SO2. Note: some individuals (e.g. asthmatics) are particularly susceptible to SO2. Yeast metabolism under “stress” conditions (lack of nutrients) – also, some strains more susceptible. Results in the production of H2S and/or other sulfur containing chemicals. Also the result of yeast autolysis – breakdown of dead yeast cells if cider is not racked. Chemical oxidation of cider/perry – stored cider or perry in contact with air or containing high levels of dissolved oxygen. More likely to be found in cider/perry stored in small wooden barrels or in PET bottles kept past their shelf-life date. Diacetyl (Extreme) Aroma/Flavor Typically described as:

- “Buttery, butterscotch, buttermilk, honey,

- Vicinal diketones (VDK)

o Commonly produced in the early stages of fermentation. o A number of species and strains of lactic acid bacteria can also produce very large amounts of vicinal diketones. As with a number of other so-called ‘spoilage’ compounds, low levels will positively contribute to the aroma and flavor of cider.

- “Excrement, faecal, shitty, dog mess” Not

sulfurous. Can also be floral and /or perfumed (low concentrations) or like mothballs (high concentrations). Produced by some yeasts during their metabolism Phenolic (Extreme) Aroma/flavor Typically described as:

- “Antiseptic, TCP, hospital-like, sticking

plasters, band-aid, carbolic, medicinal, pharmaceutical, iodine-like” Also:

- “Barnyard, wet wool, burnt beans, horsey”

Volatile phenols – notably ethyl phenol and ethyl catechol – are commonly found in the more traditional bittersweet ciders. They are produced from “tannins” – e.g. chlorogenic acid - found naturally in cider apples. Also produced by lactic acid bacteria (during malo-lactic fermentation). At high levels – e.g. as a result of “wild yeast” contamination and activity – the aroma and flavour is unpleasant as indicated. TCP can also be produced chemically when chlorine in water reacts with apple tannins. Cider Sickness Aroma/flavor Visual o A sweet and pungent “banana-skin” aroma. Also: “Rotten banana skins, raspberry”. o Sometimes: “bruised apples, emulsion paint, acetaldehyde, aldehyde” o Also, often accompanied with the aroma of hydrogen sulfide (“bad eggs”) o Visually – a dense, milky-white turbidity or haze will often be present. o Not uncommon in traditional French bittersweet ciders made using maceration & keeving. o Mainly caused by the bacteria Zymomonas anaerobia in stored cider where there is residual sugar and a low acid concentration. These bacteria produce high levels of acetaldehyde and H2S. Mousiness Flavor After-taste Flavor not immediately apparent, but builds up and is usually ‘detected’ at the back of the mouth/ throat after the cider/perry has been swallowed. Flavor commonly lingers for some time after the cider has been consumed. Flavor typically described as:

- “Mousy, mouse cage, rat-like, rodent, rodent

droppings” Flavour also described as:

- “Bready, bready-beer, pop-corn, fresh-baked,

biscuit-like”. Can be produced by species of lactic acid bacteria and (uncommonly) strains of yeasts. Some people are more (genetically) susceptible (high oral pH) to the off-flavour than others. Less susceptible individuals usually require training to be able to detect the problem. Citrus (Extreme) Flavor/aroma o “Citrus, grapefruit, lemon, marmalade” o Usually the result of fermenting or storing cider/perry in ex-fruit-juice concentrate containers, plastic drums or the large 1500 litre “Black-Plastic” vats.

- Too acidic (sour);

- Flabby or oversweet – lacking acidity.

- Too bitter; too astringent; tannic; harsh.

- Thin.

An imbalance in the ‘normal’ or ‘natural’ flavor profile of cider. An individual flavor compound “stands out” too much and detracts from overall flavor-balance of the product. Can also be:

- Flat (under-carbonated)

- Gassy (over carbonated)

- Characterless

Inappropriate blend/use of apples; Poor blending and/or product make-up. Flabbiness can also be found in cider/perry that has been in storage for too long Off-Flavors (Other) Flavor/aroma Other than those listed above, a number of other off-flavors and/or aromas can also be found in cider and perry. These include:

- Soapiness – “goaty, tallowy, horse-like”

- Cheesy – “stale cheese, old hops”

- Rancid – “Rancid, rancid butter”

- Mouldy – “Musty, earthy, damp soil, leaf

mould, fusty, cellar-like”

- Acrolein - a compound that produces a harsh,

unpleasant bitterness. Also:

- Saccharin – “Artificial, bitterness with

sweetness” Metabolites produced by (notably) lactic acid bacteria and (also) certain strains of ‘wild’ yeast. Excess use of additives – notably, saccharin.
