# Final Processing & Packaging

> Source: Peter Mitchell, *Cider & Perry Production — A Foundation* (2nd ed., 2021), Cider Institute of North America. D. Making Cider & Perry — Stage Two: Final Processing & Packaging, pages 56–58.

After primary filtration, blending and final product make-up, the final stages of processing may include some of the following activities (depending on individual requirements) prior to packaging: o Carbonation (as above) o Cartridge filtration e.g. polishing o Sterile filtration o Pasteurisation


## Cartridge Filtration


For cider filtration, cartridges are offered for a number of applications, using a filter housing sited just before the filler:

- Trap – As a safe-guard to remove any debris that may have contaminated the final blend after primary filtration

- Polishing – As a final filtration step to makes sure that the cider that meets the required degree of clarity

- Guard - Used to protect final membranes when sterile filtration is being carried out

- Sterilization – Sterile filtration can only be effectively undertaken using an absolute-rated (typically 0.45µm) and

integrity testable filter cartridge. These types of cartridges are not cheap!


## In-Pack Microbiological Stability


To avoid any unwanted microbiological activity in the final packaged product and to assure that it can have more than just one or two weeks of shelf-life in the final pack, then all yeasts and bacteria need to be killed or removed from the cider during final processing and packaging. Only ONE viable yeast cell in a bottle of cider can cause problems, which will typically lead to a product recall. Some microorganisms do not require the presence of sugar for them to grow and be active. It is also important to note that the addition of SO2, even in conjunction of sorbates will not prevent microbial activity. Thus, the two main techniques that are commonly used are either sterile filtration or (heat) pasteurization.


## Sterile Filtration


The major advantage of a sterile filter cartridge (typically 0.45µm; absolute rated) is that it can be tested before and after a filtration run (so called “integrity testing”) and, providing the membrane is integral, it can assure the quality of cider filtered. .… BUT ….. All items of equipment downstream of the sterile filter unit (pipe work, filler etc) will also need to be sterile (i.e. complete absence of any microorganisms). In addition, the pack (e.g. bottle & caps) will also need to be sterile before being filled and the downstream processes used (e.g. bottle handling between filling and capping) must ensure that NO microbial contamination can result. In other words, “aseptic packaging” is required. In addition, regular and routine microbiological testing is required, which typically means that on-site resources are required. All of this is neither easy or cheap!


## Pasteurization


Pasteurization, involving the use of heat is an alternative to sterile filtration (Note: UV pasteurization is NOT effective against yeasts). The main points to appreciate are that: o The temperature of the product being treated in pasteurization must exceed the minimum lethal temperature of the microorganisms concerned. For example and based on current evidence and effective practice, the absolute minimum temperature for cider must be above 600 C.

o As outlined above, the total population of microorganisms found in cider do not all die at the same time once the lethal temperature is reached. The greater the number of microorganisms present, the longer it takes for pasteurization to be effective at any defined temperature. o So, considering both the above points: o The higher the temperature of pasteurization, the faster the microbial population will be reduced and …. o The longer the heating time the more micro-organisms will be killed …….. so …… o A simple equation is therefore used to calculate the amount of pasteurization provided: Lethal Value (Units: PU) = Time (mins) x 1.393 Temperature – 60 (Temperature in oC) o It should also be recognised that pasteurization can never result in absolute sterility in the treated product concerned, but – in practice – it can ensure sufficient “microbial kill” and thus assure microbiological stability in- pack. o Thus the major issue with pasteurization is that it does not produce a sterile product. If the initial microbial loading is high enough, organisms will survive. It is therefore important to keep the initial microbial loading down as far as is possible to ensure effective heat treatment. There are three main types of pasteurization: 1. Hot Filling. This is only suitable for non-carbonated products filled into the larger containers such as two-plus liter flagons and bag-in-boxes. The product is heated then filled into the container and will remain hot for an extended period of time to ensure sufficient microbial kill. 2. Flash Pasteurization. Upstream of the filler, the cider is heated to a relatively high temperature (800 C) in a plate heat exchanger, before being chilled down again after just a few seconds. As with sterile filtration, aseptic packaging must then be used. This is the only type of pasteurization suitable for keg cider (in addition to sterile filtration), but it is essential that the keg to be filled is also sterile! 3. In-Pack Pasteurization. Commonly carried out with canned cider and also very useful (and simple to use) for the smaller producer. The filled and sealed pack of cider (still or carbonated) is heated up either in a water-bath pasteurizer or cabinet (chamber) pasteurizer or a tunnel pasteurizer. As an initial guide for hot filling and in-pack pasteurization, a Lethal Value of 50 – 60 PUs may be sufficient (e.g. 63 0 C for 20 minutes) for cider pasteurized in these ways. For flash pasteurization, then a minimum of 150 PUs should be aimed for. However, it is critically important that the heating regime is properly validated for each product to be pasteurized i.e. trials should be carried out using a specified heating regime. The treated products should then being sent for microbiological analysis to check that the treatment has been effective. A Chamber Pasteuriser


## Packaging


As the old saying goes, “The package is the product”! A number of options are available and include: o Glass bottles o Plastic (PET) bottles o Cans o Bag-in-box o Kegs o Pouches When establishing a cider production enterprise and on an ongoing basis, this aspect requires significant thought, research, time and effort. The following main points must be considered: o The package is the first and main interface between the producer and the ultimate consumer. o The package will typically cost more than the liquid it contains. o What unit volume is suitable? If & how are the packs going to be cased? o What range of packaging is easily available? How many units are required and/or need to be obtained and stored at any one time? o How is the product going to be processed e.g. will it be carbonated and/or will in-pack pasteurisation be undertaken? o What type of labels will be applied? What type of caps will be used e.g. crown vs. Roll on Pilfer Proof (ROPP)? o How suitable is the package in supporting the brand image that is to be projected? Work with Graphic Designers will therefore normally be required. Bottle Rinser, Filler & Capping Machine
