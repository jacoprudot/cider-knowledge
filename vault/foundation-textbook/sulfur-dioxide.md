# Sulfur Dioxide in Cider Production

> Source: Peter Mitchell, *Cider & Perry Production — A Foundation* (2nd ed., 2021), Cider Institute of North America. C. Making Cider & Perry — Stage One: Fruit Selection to Maturation, pages 39–40.


## History and Background


Prior to the 20th century sulfur used to be burnt in barrels to help keep them clean. The first documented recommendation for use in cider can be found in Evelyn’s Pomona in 1664, although it had been previously used by the Romans. Subsequently, detailed research into the use of sulfur dioxide (SO2) in cider making was carried out in the early 1950’s at Long Ashton. The main findings were:

- Most non-fermenting yeasts and spoilage bacteria removed;

- Any added yeast cultures could then dominate fermentation and impose their own flavour characteristics

SO2 is still widely used in cider production and its controlled use is highly recommended. Advantages of using SO2 a) Kills off many spoilage organisms, which can either result in specific infections and the production of unwanted taints. b) Promotes a more efficient yeast fermentation either by ‘wild’ or selected yeasts c) It also has anti-oxidant properties and thus can help extend the shelf-life of packaged products d) Helps prevent chill hazes. e) Overall, it can result in a more standardized and predictable product being made. Disadvantages of using SO2 a) Some asthmatics are very sensitive to SO2. WHO recommend a maximum intake of 100mg per day for adults. This amount would be achieved by drinking a liter of cider containing 100mgl-1. However, it should be noted that SO2 is a widely used preservative in other foodstuffs. b) Can inhibit a malo-lactic fermentation (MLF) by suppressing lactic acid bacteria, if MLF is required. c) Destroys Thiamine (vitamin B1). d) A precursor for the possible formation of hydrogen sulfide (H2S – a “rotten-egg” smell) by yeast during fermentation. All packaged products for retail sale require a sulfite declaration on the label if the product contains more than 10mgl-1 Total SO2. Even if SO2 is not added, yeasts can produce up to about 60 mgl-1 of the compound.


## Use of Sulfur Dioxide


When SO2 is added to juice or cider, then part of it will become bound up with other substances in the liquid with the rest remaining ‘Free’. Only that which is free is effective against micro-organisms. For example, juice from rotten fruit contains substances that will bind SO2 and thus will reduce its effectiveness. Furthermore, there is also a direct relationship between the pH of the juice or cider and the microbiologically ‘active’ molecular’ SO2 (a component of the free SO2). Essentially, the lower the pH, the less total SO2 is required to achieve its antimicrobial activity. It is commonly used at three main stages of the cider production process: 1. Prior to fermentation primarily to suppress wild yeasts & bacteria. 25-150 mgl-1 total SO2 is added dependant on pH & whether or not the juice has been pasteurised. 2. On racking for the control of microflora during post-fermentation storage/maturation. More is added at this stage of the production process to target (typically) 30– 40 mgl-1 Free SO2. 3. On bottling. Its primary purpose at this stage is to minimize oxidation in the final pack and thus help extend shelf-life. It should be recognised that SO2, even in conjunction with Sorbates (another preservative that can be used) cannot be relied upon to suppress microbial growth and activity in pack. There are yeasts that are extremely resistant to SO2. SO2 itself is a gas and is a significant health and safety risk if it is used like this, so it is usually added in the form of a solution of Sodium Metabisulfite (SMS) or Potassium Metabisulfite (KMS). SMS contains about 65% w/w available SO2 and KMS about 55 - 57% w/w SO 2, therefore these concentrations need to be taken into account when adding the appropriate compound to juice or cider or making a stock solution as outlined below.

One simple way to use sulfur dioxide is to produce a 10% w/v solution (as SO2). Using KMS, accurately weigh and dissolve it in distilled or de-ionized water and make up to a final total volume of ONE liter. The resulting solution should be stored safely in a labeled stoppered bottle and will have a shelf life of up to three months. 1 milliliter of this solution is equivalent to 100mg of SO2.
