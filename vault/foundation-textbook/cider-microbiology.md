- Organic Acids. These compounds affect the pH and taste of the juice and cider. The main acid in apples is Malic

acid (1.0 – 14 gl-1), but apples also contain some: Quinic acid 0.4 – 5 gl-1 Citric < 0.2 gl-1 This acid can be found at higher concentrations in pear juice. Citramalic < 0.5 gl-1

- Nitrogenous Compounds. The amount of soluble nitrogen can typically vary from almost zero to about 350 mg per

litre and possibly higher in apples from heavily fertilized bush-orchards. The soluble nitrogen is mainly in the form of Amino-acids (primarily aspartic acid, glumatic acid and asparagine), with (unlike grapes) only trace amounts of inorganic nitrogen being present. These compounds are needed by the yeast as a primary nutrient.

- Phenolics (Tannins). The main ones are Chlorogenic acid, Phloridzin, Epicatechin, Procyanidin. All apples contain

phenolics, but as detailed above, cider apples have much higher concentrations of these chemical compounds and therefore have a significant impact on the flavour of the apples and cider.

- Aroma Compounds. Although overall at very low concentrations compared with the other components of apple

juice, these compounds provide the aroma and contribute to the flavour of apples. Between 80 – 90% of the aroma compounds found in apples are esters (e.g. ethyl acetate), with the particular mix determining the different aroma and flavour of each individual apple variety. Additionally – particularly in the aromatic apples – there are also a number of alcohols e.g. hexanol and trace amounts of aldehydes e.g. acetaldehyde. There is limited evidence to show that the original aroma compounds found in apples carry-through to the fermented cider and thus most of the aroma compounds found in cider are yeast-derived.

- ‘B’ Group Vitamins. Although essential for yeast growth and activity (e.g. Vitamin B1 – thiamine and vitamin B5 –

panthothenate), the concentration of these compounds vary considerably between varieties and seasons.

- Mineral Salts. Along with nitrogenous compounds and vitamins, minerals are essential for yeast growth and activity.

Variable amounts can be found in apple juice, correlated with the amount of the appropriate fertilizer added to the trees in the orchard.

- Pectin. This is a jelly-forming substance that originates in the cell walls of apples and pears. It becomes soluble as

the apple and pear ripens and is liberated when the fruit is milled. It thus can cause juice extraction problems when pressing the mash if found at a high concentration.

Cider making is a biotechnological process and, as such, the growth and activity of microorganisms are central to production. Simply put, the job of a cider maker is a microbiological controller. All readers are therefore strongly encouraged to gain as much knowledge and understanding about microbiology and biochemistry as possible (and continue to learn), in order to facilitate the production of high quality cider and perry on an ongoing basis and confidently deal with challenges as they arise and before they become problems.


## Fundamentals of Microbiology


A few basic principles first: 1. Two main types of microorganisms can be found in cider and will, if present and active, have an impact on the product: Yeasts, which are a type of fungi and bacteria, which are a simple group of prokaryotic organisms in their own right. Microorganisms are typically given Latin names e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the main yeast species that carries out the primary alcoholic fermentation and Lactobacillus collinoides, a rod-shaped species of bacteria that may cause a secondary “malo-lactic fermentation” but unfortunately can also produce off-flavours. 2. Micro-organisms are very small: Yeasts measure about 5 – 40µm in diameter, where as bacteria are typically about 1/10th of this and range from 0.5 – 5.0µm in size. Yeast cells are often ellipsoid in shape, but there are some that look different from this. Bacteria are normally either spherical (“cocci”) or rod-like (“bacilli”) but, again, different shapes can be found, though not commonly in cider.

3. Microbiology is a ‘numbers game’. The presence by itself of one single microbial cell will not have very much impact (positive or negative) on the environment (e.g. cider) in which it exists, but the more there are the greater effect they will have. For example, 100 million yeast cells per millilitre are required for fermentation to progress satisfactorily, whereas in many packaged ciders the requirement is to have none at all. It is therefore more common to talk about microbial populations - “Total Viable Count” (TVC) or “microbial loading”. 4. Following on from above, microbial growth is mainly defined as an increase in viable cell numbers – one cell divides into two, two into four etc - and will happen in an exponential fashion if the conditions are conducive for the individual species concerned e.g. if the conditions are ideal, one yeast cell can grow into 10 million cells in 24 hours!. The main factors that affect the ability of microorganisms to grow and survive are as follows: a. Availability of water – if not present, microorganisms will typically not grow, but some can remain dormant. Usually, there is plenty of water available in the cider making process. b. Some microorganisms require oxygen for growth and are termed as being “Aerobic” (e.g. Acetic Acid bacteria). For some microorganisms, oxygen is actually toxic (“Obligate Anaerobes”), but none of these are associated with cider production. Others are “Facultative Anaerobes” and are able to thrive whether oxygen is present or not, however they may ‘perform’ differently depending on the conditions e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae requires oxygen for growth, but it will only start producing alcohol in anaerobic conditions. c. As living organisms, a range of nutrients are also required for growth and activity e.g. sugar as a source of energy and carbon skeletons, a nitrogen source for the production of proteins, along with vitamins & minerals. d. A suitable temperature. Most organisms will not grow and thrive if the temperature is too low and then will die if a high (“lethal”) temperature is reached. Within the normal ‘comfort zone’, the higher the temperature the quicker the organisms will grow and the more active they will become (e.g. a faster fermentation). In cider production, this so-called ‘comfort zone’ typically ranges from 100 – 300 C, but is species dependant. e. Microorganisms are significantly affected by the pH of the environment. Many yeasts and bacteria cannot grow and survive at the relatively low pH found in juice and cider. The lower the pH is the more challenging is the environment for the organisms concerned. f. There are a number of chemical agents that have anti-microbial properties. The most important example of this is sulphur dioxide (SO2). Some of the microorganisms found in juice or cider are very susceptible to SO2 (e.g. Kloeckera apiculata) and others are not (e.g. Saccharomycodes ludwigii). Phenolics also have some anti-microbial properties, as does alcohol itself. Other anti-microbial agents are also permitted for use in cider, notably sorbates and dimethyldicarbonate (“Velcorin”). 5. When there is an existing population of viable microorganisms, when lethal temperatures are reached, the microorganisms do not all die at once; they die (just like they grow) in an exponential fashion. The higher the population density the longer it therefore takes for ‘microbial kill’ to occur. This is the background to heat pasteurisation. Theoretically, however, absolutely sterility can never be attained, but in practice sufficient microbial kill can be achieved to ensure that the treated product will be microbiologically stable. 6. Microorganisms can be found everywhere, but juice and cider should be viewed as a ‘selective growth media’ (mainly due to its relatively low pH) and thus only a limited number of different types will be found in juice and cider. Micro-organisms in Cider Production – An Overview


## Yeast species


Surveys have identified a wide range of yeasts that can be found on apples, but many are of no importance. Those of significance found on the fruit and, therefore, in the fresh pressed juice include: Kloeckera apiculata, Saccharomycodes ludwigii and Candida sp The main yeast responsible for fermentation is Saccharomyces cerevisiae. This yeast is rarely found on apples but gets into the juice from plant and equipment, or added cultures. Each yeast species can have a variety of strains, with each one having different characteristics. Overall, the main yeasts that can be found in cider and may affect the sensory properties of the product are as follows:

- Saccharomyces cerevisiae

- Kloeckera apiculata (Hanseniaspora uvarum)

- Candida stellata (Torulopsis stellata, T. bacillaris)

- Metschnikowia pulcherrima (Candida plucherrima, Torulopsis pulcherrima)

- The “Film” yeasts: Pichia membranaefaciens & Candida valida. Also, Pichia fermentans & Hansenula

anomala

- Dekkera bruxellensis (Brettanomyces intermedius). Imperfect form: B. Bruxellensis

- Saccharomyces ludwigii

- Zygosaccharomyces balii & Z. rouxii

- Rhodotorula spp.

Many, but not all of the above yeasts can have a negative impact on the sensory properties of cider and have therefore been identified as potential spoilage organisms.


## Bacteria


There are a range of bacteria that also can have a significant effect on cider and perry. Most, but not all, are potentially spoilage organisms. Important ones are acid and alcohol tolerant and include:

- Acetic acid bacteria:

- Acetobacter spp. - overoxidisers

- Gluconobacter (Acetomonas) spp. – underoxidisers

- Zymononas mobilis (var. mobilis and var. pomacii)

- Lactic acid bacteria:

- Streptocuccus spp.

- Leuconostoc spp.

- Oenococcus spp.

- Pediococcus spp.

- Lactobacillus spp.

- (Bacillus spp.)

Overall, it should be recognised that cider and perry do tend to be very susceptible to microbial spoilage (humans are not the only living organisms that particularly like apple-based products!) and by their nature, the products do not ‘hide’ faults very well either.
