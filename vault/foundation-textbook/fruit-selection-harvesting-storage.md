MAKING CIDER & PERRY – STAGE ONE: FRUIT SELECTION TO MATURATION Fruit Selection for Cider & Perry Production – Key Considerations Cider: ü Most apple varieties are suitable for making cider! ü A blend of apples tends to work best (3 – 4 varieties processed together). A reasonable level of acidity is required (important for pH control), but not too much. Aromatic apples can lead to more complex flavours in the ciders being made but it is important to note that most of the aroma components found in cider will be derived from the metabolic activity of yeasts (& bacteria) and will not carry-over from the original fruit. ü For tannin (bitterness, astringency, a greater ‘body’ in the cider and more aroma complexity) then cider apples should be used. Although crab apples (& Quince!) can be used, care should be taken not to use too many as harsh flavours can be produced in the final cider. Perry: ü It can be difficult to make good perry with dessert pears as some varieties contain aroma compounds that are unpleasant in the fermented perry. Often better results can be obtained using dessert pear juice concentrate, as the concentration process removes these undesirable components. ü With Perry pears, however, potential white wine-like qualities can be achieved.


## Harvesting


As introduced above, it is important to harvest apples at the correct stage of ripeness. If apples are unripe:

- The sugar content will be low

- They typically contain high starch levels

- The flavour will not be fully developed

- (The acid levels will be high)

When to Harvest Apples 1. Fruit can be readily shaken from trees 2. Pips (seeds) brown 3. Waxy skin 4. Use starch test: Immerse a cut apple in an iodine solution. If unripe, starch content evenly spread across whole surface. As the apple matures, the starch disappears from the central portion, leaving only a small amount on the periphery. 5. Measure sugar levels


## Fruit Storage


Many varieties of cider apples, if harvested carefully with little or no bruising, will benefit from a period of storage prior to processing. Potential benefits include an increase in Specific Gravity (SG) and improvements in flavour as a result of oxidation of the apple phenolics: Breakwells seedling: Maximum 3 weeks Tremlett’s Bitter: Maximum 3 weeks Dabinett: 3-4 weeks Yarlington Mill: 3-4 weeks Michelin: 3-4 weeks Kingston Black: 3-4 weeks It is recognized, however, that this is not often practicable particularly on a large commercial scale. Care is also needed with some fruit as it can get difficult to press if over ripe.

Fruit Quality The size and shape of the fruit and presence of skin blemishes or surface cracking on apples and pears for cider/perry production does not cause any problems. Bruising itself is also not a problem, but can rapidly lead to rotting. However the use of rotten fruit should be avoided as far as is possible, as this will be a significant source of unwanted microbial contamination, it will reduce the effectiveness of any sulphur dioxide being used (see below) and will make the fruit more difficult to press. It should be noted, based on evidence to date, potential food pathogens (e.g. E. coli) do not survive the fermentation process and the micro-toxin patulin that can also be found on rotten apples and is an established and regulated potential contaminant of apple juice, is metabolized during cider making by the fermentation yeast to the less toxic ascladiol. Milling & Pressing After delivery to the Cider Mill, the first stage of the cider making process is to mill the fruit into a fine pulp (“mash”) prior to juice extraction in a press. Most fruit, however, unless it has been handpicked and is completely clean, will benefit from washing prior to processing. Water-based handling systems will also provide a mechanism for fruit transport and trapping stones - apples float and stones sink! Unfortunately, pears also sink, so perry producers will modify fruit handling systems accordingly. Mills should be matched to the capacity of the press and, ideally, should be adjustable in order to produce varying sizes of mash particles as determined by the type of fruit being processed. Typically, with high tannin cider apples, the fruit can be ground very finely, however with high pectin dessert fruit, better juice yields will be obtained when the apples or pears are more coarsely ground. There are a number of different types of cider presses in current use. Typical juice yields are between 650 to 850 liters per tonne, dependent on the variety of fruit processed (e.g. cider apples tend to have higher yields than dessert apples) and the type of press being used. After juice extraction, the solid residue left is called “pomace”, usually sent to farmers for use in feeding cattle and pigs, or used in biomass energy systems. The main types of presses are as follows: 1. Batch Presses (Pressure) a) Bed press / hydraulic ram press / pack press / rack and cloth press “Cheeses” are built up between slatted racks, which are configured in a vertical stack (e.g. Voran pack press) or horizontally (e.g. Goodnature presses) High juice yields, but labor intensive. Typically used by smaller-scale producers. b) Horizontal cage presses e.g. Bucher HP The press is repeatedly filled and pressed until press chamber is 80% full. The outer casing and piston are withdrawn and the cylinder rotated to discharge pomace. During filling, hydraulics are turned off, piston pushed back by the mash being pumped in. During pressing, the juice will flow through sleeved channels. High juice yields, high capital cost, high power requirements, fully automated. Used by many of the large-scale producers.

2. Continuous Presses (Pressure) a) Belt (band) presses e.g. Voran The mash is squeezed between continuous belts (either between rollers or by belt tension). The juice flows through the perforated bottom belt and pomace removed by scraper / auger. Lower juice yields than hydraulic presses and are typically more difficult to keep clean, but are much cheaper than horizontal cage presses. These types of press are becoming increasingly popular with medium-scale producers. Other fruit processing treatments 1. Use of press-aids: Addition of inert cellulose-based materials (e.g. rice hulls) after milling and prior to pressing can improve juice extraction particularly with ripe dessert pears or high-pectin dessert apples. 2. Hot water diffusion: The pressed pomace is broken up and washed with hot water to extract residual phenolics and sugars. 3. Enzymes: If required, pectinase can be added to the mash and/or pressed juice to remove pectin. Amylase can also be added to the juice if residual starch is present Juice Treatment After the juice is extracted, samples are commonly sent to the on-site laboratory for analysis. Cider producers, both large and small, should have equipment to undertake certain basic tests. The fresh-pressed juice should be checked for its sugar concentration (usually indirectly by measuring its Specific Gravity (S.G.)), pH and the concentration of acid. The adjustment of pH to a defined level (possibly by adding food-grade acid to a pH below 3.8), is key to ensuring that a successful and efficient fermentation is achieved without spoilage and the production of off-flavours by unwanted micro- organisms. The addition of sulphur dioxide to fresh-pressed juice is also typically carried out (see below).


## Apple & Pear Juice Concentrate


The cider industry uses three main types of concentrate:

- Dessert / Culinary apple at between 15 – 40gl-1 acidity

- Bittersweet apple at between 15 - 25gl-1 acidity

- Pear

If concentrate is to be used, then the following points should be considered: o It should be purchased from a reputable supplier. There is, unfortunately, a lot of badly made (and cheap!) concentrate available, but a well-made concentrate can be fermented to a very good cider. o Ideally, store at 40 – 10°C for a maximum of 12 months. Keep the container sealed to avoid water condensing on the surface. o A diluted sample should be assessed: o Meets specification (as required) e.g. usually at 700 Brix / SG 1.350 o The taste & flavour should be acceptable i.e. apple/pear-like, not burnt or caramelized and with fermented or other off-flavours. o Typically, it should be diluted with potable water to “standard” juice strength prior to use (to SG 1.045; a 6-fold dilution from SG 1.350) and the pH adjusted accordingly. o Initially mix 50:50 with hot water (add concentrate to water) and mix with air or re-circulating pump or with a mechanical mixer.
