# Blending & Carbonation

> Source: Peter Mitchell, *Cider & Perry Production — A Foundation* (2nd ed., 2021), Cider Institute of North America. D. Making Cider & Perry — Stage Two: Final Processing & Packaging, pages 54–55.


## It’s all in the Blending!



## Overview


On its own, ‘straight’ fermented apple juice (dry base cider) is too challenging for the majority of consumers, thus it usually needs blending with other ingredients, such as sugar. In summary, there are two main options for obtaining a sweetened cider: 1. Arrested fermentation to leave fruit-derived residual sugar (fructose, sucrose & glucose) in the cider. It must then be remembered that the resulting cider is microbiologically unstable unless steps are taken to prevent subsequent microbiological activity. There are two ways that arrested fermentation can be achieved: a. Maceration & Keeving - the traditional English or French method. Not an easy technique to carry- out reliably and, typically, the cider remains microbiologically unstable, so best consumed quickly (as commonly practiced in France!) before the cider spoils. b. Use of modern technology e.g. chilling, filtration & pasteurisation of part-fermented cider. 2. Back-sweetening the dry base cider with added sugar (fructose or sucrose are commonly used), fresh apple juice or apple juice concentrate. (Note: Juice also contains organic acids, so it is often difficult to achieve a suitable sugar / acid balance if only juice is used to back-sweeten) This is the most common technique used, which has an additional advantage in that the fully attenuated ‘dry’ base cider can be left for a period of time to mature before it is back- sweetened prior to packaging.


## The Blending Process


Blending & product make-up involves mixing different base ciders and (either at the same time or subsequently) addition of other selected ingredients to a recipe, thus producing a final product specification as follows: + %ABV (e.g. 6.0%ABV) - achieved with added ingredients and de-chlorinated potable water. + Sweetness (e.g. 26gl-1 sugar; SG = 1.010) – using sugar and/or concentrate/juice + Acidity (e.g. TA = 3.6gl-1) e.g. with malic or citric acid + Color e.g. with caramel + Antioxidant – SO2 or ascorbate + Carbon dioxide (e.g. 3.9gl-1) – addition of CO2 to produce a sparkling cider A New Product Development Team in Action

The process should always be undertaken using bench-trials first – accurately use measuring cylinders, scales etc - before scaling up to produce the final “made-up” blend for packaging. As an initial guide, the following are typical specifications for finished products:

- Dry cider: SG = 1.005; 13gl-1 sugar. Ratio of total sugar (gl-1) to total acid (gl-1): < 8:1

- Medium cider: SG = 1.010 – 1.015; 26gl-1 – 40gl-1 sugar. Ratio sugar (gl-1) to acid (gl-1): 8:1 to 12:1

- Sweet cider: SG = 1.020; 50gl-1 sugar. Ratio sugar (gl-1) to acid (gl-1): > 12:1

A number of other points should also be noted: o Sugar masks acid and tannin, thus (as indicated above) sweeter ciders do require higher levels of acidity to ensure that they are suitably balanced. o In addition to the effect of the original juice content, increasing sugar, carbonation & alcohol and decreasing acidity will tend to increase the body of a cider. o At the same level of alcohol, a drier cider will be perceived as more alcoholic than a sweeter product. o Higher carbonation tends to increase the apparent acidity and the cider will be perceived as being ‘drier’ o Try out blends on others - use consumers to help develop blends (e.g. utilize hedonic Preference Ranking test to establish the preferred blend out of 3 – 5 different recipes) and be willing to adapt blends according with ongoing consumer feedback.


## Carbonation


The majority of products in the marketplace are carbonated. Simply, this involves subjecting the cider to pressurized CO2. The lower the product temperature, the lower the gas-pressure required to carbonate the product. A simple system involves holding chilled cider in a pressure tank and injecting-in CO2 through a carbonation stone over a period of time. Agitation of cider helps the CO2 to dissolve. Alternatively, continuous systems can be used: (1) Pressure Tank – cold cider is sprayed through a tank pressurized with CO2. (2) Pin-Point – cold cider is pumped through an in-line CO2 injector. The product will then be held under pressure through to packaging, which is typically carried out in a counter-pressure filling machine. The level of carbonation is commonly specified in terms of grams per liter: 2gl-1 CO2 = 1 volume (1 liter of gas dissolved in 1 liter of liquid). Typical levels of carbonation for packaged cider are 3.5 – 5.5 gl-1 CO2.
