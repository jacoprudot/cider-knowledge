# Final Processing & Packaging — Overview & Key Considerations

> Source: Peter Mitchell, *Cider & Perry Production — A Foundation* (2nd ed., 2021), Cider Institute of North America. D. Making Cider & Perry — Stage Two: Final Processing & Packaging, pages 50–50.

D. MAKING CIDER & PERRY – STAGE TWO: FINAL PROCESSING & PACKAGING


## Overview


Depending on - first and foremost – consumer requirements, the style of cider being produced, the scale and wishes of the individual cider maker concerned and, sometimes, specified requirements of a customer (e.g. supermarket), final processing will involve some of the following activities:

- Blending and restoring of base ciders.

- Carrying out a secondary In-Bottle Fermentation to produce a ‘naturally’ sparking product.

- Filtration to produce a clarified “bright” product.

- Making-up the product according to a recipe to its final specification by, for example, adding sugar to make a

sweet cider.

- Carbonation (injection of CO2 under pressure) to produce a sparkling product.

- Pasteurisation to produce a product that will remain stable and will not spoil in the bottle, can or keg.

- Instead of pasteurisation, Sterile Filtration may be carried out. This involves passing the cider through a

membrane filter, which is guaranteed to remove all yeasts and bacteria – thus rendering the final product sterile.

- Packaging the cider into a glass or plastic PET bottle, metal can, cask, keg, flagon or bag-in-box.

- Label application and casing into boxes or onto shrink-wrap trays.


## Key Considerations


Although - but very obviously – this second main stage of cider making is central to the success of any cider production enterprise, there are a range of choices to be made and decisions taken, not the least because the equipment and facilities required, if purchase is to be undertaken, are often extremely expensive. In addition, downstream processing and packaging is a ‘volume driven’ business, with the economies often only being achieved on a large-scale. The following main points therefore require very careful thought and consideration:

- As indicated above, usually significant capital investment is required.

- Additional technical expertise is also required.

- Specific items of equipment (e.g. a bottle filler) typically requires specialist services to operate it (e.g. three-phase

electricity, compressed air etc).

- All equipment requires regular maintenance. Compare the maintenance requirements of a tank (simple!) to that of

a labelling machine (complex).

- The products themselves require regular monitoring, for example:

o Accurate determination of alcohol content o Sterile packing requires microbiological checks o Carbonation requires CO2 checks

- An additional range of materials will be required and stored e.g. bottles, labels, CO2 etc.

A number of potential options for undertaking this work also require careful consideration by the cider maker as follows: 1. Undertake all of the activities ‘in-house’. 2. Undertake some of the work ‘in-house’ (e.g. blending & filtration) and get others to do the rest (e.g. bottling). 3. Sub-contract all of the work to someone else. 4. Rent equipment/facilities if available. 5. Form and operate a co-operative or collaborate with others to share equipment and resources. 6. Develop alternative approaches to marketing or presenting the cider to the consumer e.g. as a cloudy and still product (no need to filter or carbonate). Thus limit the range of required process steps, but this is likely to require considerable ‘consumer education’! Downstream processing & packaging can be viewed as “Unit Operation ”, with all the individual activities interlinked and being undertaken over a relatively short period of time (often within just one day).
