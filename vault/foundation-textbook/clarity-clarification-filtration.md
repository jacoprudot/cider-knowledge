## Cider Clarity & Clarification


If untreated, post-maturation cider will (to some degree) be cloudy. This cloudiness will be affected by:

- Fruit variety: Cider produced from cider apples will often be less cloudy than that produced from dessert/culinary

apples.

- Length of storage time: Normally, the longer the cider is stored, the clearer it will be.

- Microbial loading.

Many consumers prefer or expect that the cider that they purchase will be clear, but (if felt appropriate) it may be a possibility to sell a cloudy product as long as it is clearly specified as such. Clarification can involve some or all of the following processes:


## Fining


By definition, fining is the addition of a reactive substance that will remove unwanted components (e.g. cloudy particles). Typically a fining agent has an electrical charge and will thus attract and remove others of the opposite charge. A number of agents can be used, either alone or in conjunction (in sequence) with others. It is unlikely that the final degree of product clarity will be achieved just by using fining agents, but it will reduce the need for some filtration. The main agents that can be used are as follows:

- Bentonite – Has a negative charge, and thus will attract and remove yeasts and proteins, which are positively

charged.

- Gelatin – Has a positive charge, and thus will attract and remove bacteria and phenolics (tannin) compounds that

are negatively charged.

- Bentonite + Gelatin can be used in conjunction with each other: Bentonite first, then followed up with gelatin

- Chitin – Although it has a positive charge, it is a general flocculating agent. But, as it is made from ground crab

shells, the use of it may require a label declaration as a potential allergen.

- Other possible agents include isinglass (made from fish swim bladders), kieselsol (silica) and potassium casienate

(made from milk protein). The main principles involved to achieve successful fining are:

- The agent will firstly require thorough mixing with water (and sometimes other ingredients e.g. malic acid for

bentonite) to produce a suspension of defined concentration (e.g. 5%w/v). Follow manufacturer’s instructions.

- The suspension should then be allowed to stand for the specified time: 4 – 24 hours dependent on the agent used.

- Fining trials must always be run just before each usage to determine the ideal dose to be used. Trials are best done

in measuring cylinders and at the same temperature as the fining is to be carried out. The objective is to identify the minimum dose necessary to achieve an acceptable result. Avoid over-fining.

- Once the dose rate has been established (often after just 2 or 3 hours of testing), the suspension must be added

slowly to the cider and mixed in well.

- The liquid then must be allowed to stand undisturbed

- Keep the temperature constant

- Whenever possible use a tall narrow vessel


## Centrifugation


Essentially, only applicable in large-scale production and is not often used these days. The process is not really suitable for small-scale producers. Centrifugation separates two phases by density, with the degree and speed of separation dependent on the force of gravity achieved in the centrifuge.


## Filtration


There are three main types of primary filtration as follows: 1. Sheet / Pad Filtration The filter sheets (pads) are typically made from cellulose, come in a number of different grades and are held between the support plates of the unit itself. The pads work as “depth” filters and retain the particles in three ways: (1) Trapping the particle on the surface or within the matrix of the pad (“media”) as it is too big to pass through the holes or channels of the media. (2) Retention, by impaction (“inertial impaction”), within the pad itself. (3) The filter media will sometimes have an electrical charge (“Zeta” potential) and will thus attract and retain particles of an opposite electrical charge. As all pads are ‘nominally’ rated, even using the ‘tightest’ grade (i.e. has the smallest hole), it is absolutely NOT possible to sterile filter the cider using a pad filter. 2. Powder Filters All require the use of a “Filter Aid” – the powder - usually diatomaceous earth. This is laid down on the filter support firstly as an initial “pre-coat” then more powder is added (“body feed”) along with the cider to be filtered. There are different grades of powder available, but again it is impossible to sterile filter using this technology. 3. Cross-Flow Filtration The un-clarified cider is circulated parallel (tangentially) to a membrane surface. As the flow is very turbulent it keeps the membrane from blocking or fouling. The filter will take the cider from the raw state to an almost sterile product in one step. Microfiltration – typically used in cross-flow filtration - uses a micron-rated pore size and removes solids from suspension by size exclusion. Typically, pore size is 0.2 micron nominal. Disadvantages o Time consuming turnaround. o Labor intensive. o Filter sheet edges exposed. o Not ideally suited to CIP. o Quality can be impaired if flow rates are varied. o Not suited to automation. Advantages o Low cost filter media – cheapest material available. o On large scale, very economical. o Fast filtration / high capacity. o Can be automated (large units). o Anaerobic operation. o Suitable for CIP. Disadvantages o H&S (powder handling). The powder is now classified as a potential carcinogen in some countries of the world. o The smaller units are not easy to operate and take a lot of experience and skills development to operate effectively. o The spent powder can only be disposed of by land-fill. o Quality can be impaired if flow rates are varied. o Significantly more expensive equipment than pad filter units.

Primary filtration can be undertaken either before blending & final product make-up (as below) or afterwards, depending on what ingredients require filtration. For example, pure white sugar may not need filtration and any water added typically also does not require it either, as long as the water is potable and clear.
