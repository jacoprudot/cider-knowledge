# Cider & Perry Production and Marketing — Legal Framework

> Source: Peter Mitchell, *Cider & Perry Production — A Foundation* (2nd ed., 2021), Cider Institute of North America. A. Background to Cider & Perry Production, pages 9–14.

Cider & Perry Production and Marketing – Legal Framework As with the production and marketing of all food and drink, cider and perry producers operate within a legal framework. General areas of legislation that affect cider production can be grouped as follows:

- Excise (Tax on alcohol production) and Definitions & Standards

- Food safety & composition. Includes general product safety.

- Food labelling. Weights & measures. Trade descriptions.

- Environmental legislation

- Workplace (occupational) health & safety

- Employment law

- Permissions (Alcohol sales, building new facilities etc)

Much of the legislation is also applicable to any business, not just those in the food and drink sector. However there are a number of areas that are specifically relevant to cider and perry production.


## Excise and Cider & Perry Definitions & Standards


In many countries of the world, including the USA and the UK, legal definitions of cider are primarily contained within excise (alcohol) tax legislation. In some of these countries, notably the UK, as long as the product (e.g. a fruit flavoured cider) meets acceptable and legal standards for food safety, the consumer is informed by appropriate labeling as to what they are buying and an appropriate level of excise duty is paid by the producer (e.g. a flavoured cider will be taxed as a “Made Wine”, not as “Cider”), then there really are no other restrictions as to what can be produced and sold. In certain countries of the world, however – notably Canada, France, Spain, Germany – definitions & practices are much more restricted by specific vertical legislation e.g. for the French “Cidre Bouche”. In the USA although the definition of cider is essentially contained within excise tax regulation (see below), as “Hard Cider” is classified as a type of wine there are also restrictions on certain permitted practices and allowable ingredients (For full details, see The Code of Federal Regulations, Title 27: Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, Part 24 – Wine). In Canada, the definition of “Cider” is contained within the Food and Drug Regulations (see C.R.C., c. 870; Part B, Division 2, Section B.02.120 - Cider), which are under the Food and Drugs Act. This Act is the primary legislation governing the safety and nutritional quality of food sold in Canada. Its scope includes food labelling, advertising and claims; food standards and compositional requirements; fortification; foods for special dietary uses; food additives; chemical and microbial hazards; veterinary drug residues; packaging material; and pesticides.


## USA Definitions


Legal position/definitions are complex: Both Federal rules and individual State regulations are applicable, which also affects the final level of excise tax payable. Federal Definition In November 2001, The Department of the Treasury (Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau - TTB) finalized regulations concerning Hard Cider to create a wine excise tax category for hard cider (November 9th 2001). This was effective from 25th January 2002 and mandatory from 27th May 2002. Subsequently in December 2015, the so-called “Cider Act” was passed amending the section of the Internal Revenue Code (26 USC Section 5041) that defines hard cider. From 1st January 2017, the definition is as follows: 1. A wine, whose carbonation level does not exceed 6.4 grams per liter; 2. Is derived primarily from apples, apple juice concentrate and water, pears or pear juice concentrate and water; 3. Contains no other fruit product or fruit flavoring other than apple or pear; 4. Contains at least 0.5% and less than 8.5% alcohol by volume. The author uses every possible care in producing these guidelines but accepts no liability whatsoever in connection with it. For further details consult the relevant statutory instruments and authorities.

Broadly, however, cider is classified as a wine in the USA and thus permitted practices and allowable ingredients are generally the same as for a wine (see below). All of the following excise tax categories are applicable to ciders made outside the Hard Cider definition (e.g. above 8.5% ABV), with each one having a different level of tax: 1. Wine: 0.5% to 14% ABV 2. Wine: 14.1% to 20.9% ABV 3. Wine: 21.0% to 24% ABV 4. Carbonated/Sparkling Wine: CO2 > 3.92 gl-1 5. Hard Cider: As above In addition, there is a “Fruit Wine” category (taxed inline with above categories), a “Farm Cider” category (which is exempt from a tax class subject to certain provisions: FR: 27.24.76) and reduced levels of tax for small producers (150,000 gal/yr) e.g. $0.056 credit on 1st 100,000 gallons of hard cider. US State Definitions Most States have adopted almost the same definition as in the Federal Internal Revenue Code (above), however there are some differences. One such example is as follows:

- Since 15th January 2014, the New York State definition of cider is:

“Cider” means the partially or fully fermented juice of fresh, whole apples or other pome fruits, containing more than 3.2% ABV but not more than 8.5%ABV: (i) to which nothing has been added to increase the alcoholic content produced by natural fermentation; and (ii) with the usual cellar treatments and necessary additions to correct defects due to climate, saccharine levels and seasonal conditions. Nothing contained in this subdivision shall be deemed to preclude the use of such methods or materials as may be necessary to encourage a normal alcoholic fermentation and to make a product that is free of microbiological activity at the time of sale. Cider may be sweetened or flavored after fermentation with fruit juice, fruit juice concentrate, sugar, maple syrup, honey, spices or other agricultural products, separately or in combination. Cider may contain retained or added carbon dioxide. (NYS Alcoholic Beverage Control (ABC) Law Article 1; Sect. 3-7b) All producers in the USA are therefore strongly advised to contact their own State authorities for clarification with respect to each individual State’s definition, detailed requirements and the appropriate level of taxation payable. USA: Product Standards, Permitted Practices and Allowable Ingredients (An Outline) As further detailed in the Code of Federal Regulations, Title 27: Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, Part 24.246 – Wine and Part 4 - Labelling and Advertising of Wine, there are a number of controls on the production of cider, additives used and its final standards. Notably these include: As part of the cider production process o Chapitalization: Sugar or concentrated apple juice (pear juice for perry) is allowed up to an amount necessary to raise the original density of the juice to no more than 250 Brix (SG: 1.105). o Type of sugar: Only pure dry sugar, liquid sugar or invert sugar syrup can be used. o Acidity (pH) Adjustment: Generally, only malic acid may be used in cider production to correct natural deficiencies to 9.0 grams per liter. There are exceptions to this and an application can be made to the TTB for the use of a different type of acid. o Calcium or Potassium (Bi)carbonate: The natural fixed acids may not be reduced below 5g/l. o (Di)ammonium Phosphate: Up to a maximum of 96 g/hl. o Calcium Pantothenate: Up to a maximum of 48 mg/hl. o Thiamine Hydrochloride: Up to a maximum of 60 mg/hl. In the finished (packaged) product o Volatile acidity not more than 1.4 grams per litre (as acetic acid) o Arsenic not more than 0.2 mg/l (OIV standard); 0.1 mg/l (Canadian standard) o Iron content not more than 7 milligrams per kilogram (UK standard) o Copper content not more than 0.5 mg/l o Lead not more than 0.15 mg/l (OIV standard) o Total SO2 not more than 350 mg/l o Sorbic Acid: No more than 300mg/l


## Canadian Definitions


In Canada, the definition of “Cider” is detailed in the Food and Drug Regulations (C.R.C., c. 870): Section B.02.120 [S]. Cider (a) shall (i) be the product of the alcoholic fermentation of apple juice, and (ii) contain not less than 2.5 per cent and not more than 13.0 per cent absolute alcohol by volume; and (b) may have the following added to it during the course of manufacture (i) yeast, (ii) concentrated apple juice, (iii) sugar, dextrose, invert sugar, glucose, glucose solids, or aqueous solutions thereof, (iv) yeast foods, (v) sulphurous acid, including salts thereof, in such quantity that its content in the finished cider shall not exceed (A) 70 parts per million in the free state, or (B) 350 parts per million in the combined state, calculated as sulphur dioxide, (vi) tartaric acid and potassium tartrate, (vii) citric acid, (viii) lactic acid, (ix) pectinase and amylase, (x) ascorbic or erythorbic acid, or salts thereof, (xi) any of the following fining agents: (A) activated carbon, (B) clay, (C) diatomaceous earth, (D) gelatin, (E) albumen, (F) sodium chloride, (G) silica gel, (H) casein, (I) tannic acid not exceeding 200 parts per million, or (J) polyvinylpyrrolidone not exceeding two parts per million in the finished product, (xii) caramel, (xiii) brandy, fruit spirit or alcohol derived from the alcoholic fermentation of a food source distilled to not less than 94 per cent alcohol by volume, (xiv) carbon dioxide, (xv) oxygen, (xvi) ozone, or (xvii) sorbic acid or salts thereof, not exceeding 500 parts per million, calculated as sorbic acid. B.02.122. [S]. Champagne Cider shall be cider that is impregnated with carbon dioxide under pressure by (a) conducting the afterpart of the fermentation in closed vessels, or (b) secondary fermentation in closed vessels with or without the addition of sugar, dextrose, invert sugar, glucose or glucose solids or aqueous solutions thereof, and shall contain not less than seven per cent absolute alcohol by volume. B.02.123. No person shall sell cider or champagne cider that has more than 0.2 per cent weight by volume (2.0gl-1) of volatile acidity calculated as acetic acid, as determined by official method FO-2, Determination of Volatile Acidity of Wine, Cider and Champagne Cider, October 15, 1981. Excise Tax in Canada Both the federal and provincial governments impose excise tax on cider and perry, which varies depending on a number of factors including the alcoholic strength, the retail price of the product concerned and the Province in which it is made and sold.


## Labelling


The main requirements for labelling are identified below, however the reader should familiarise themselves with the specific obligations of the country in which the product is to be sold 1. Common Name The words Cider or Perry must be displayed on the label Brand names and trademarks etc. cannot take the place of the above names, but may also be included. 2. Net Quantity Declaration Pre-packaged alcoholic beverages must carry on the container an indication of the net quantity, typically on the principal display panel, prominently displayed and easily definable. Acceptable symbols (usually, metric units of volume) must be used and minimum type sizes are also required. When sold otherwise than as pre-packed, the drink is required to be sold by a defined quantity. 3. Alcoholic Strength Cider and perry having an alcoholic strength of more than 0.5 %ABV must be labelled with an indication of its alcoholic strength by volume. It should be displayed on the principal display panel. The figure used should not have more than one decimal place, typically using the symbol of “% vol.” or “% alc.” Minimum type sizes are often required. Usually, a certain tolerance is allowed between the declared value on the label and the actual strength. This typically varies between +/- 0.5%ABV to +/- 1.5%ABV depending on the alcoholic strength of the specific product concerned (e.g. in Ontario, Canada: +/- 0.5% if cider is less than 5.6%ABV or +/- 1.0% for cider between 5.6 and 13% ABV). The declared value MUST, however, correctly indicate the tax status of the product concerned. 4. Name of Producer or Packer: The name and address must be shown. 5. Ingredients List & Nutrition Declaration In many countries of the world, including Canada, recognised / pre-defined (“standardized” in Canada) alcoholic beverages are normally exempt from the requirements to show a list of ingredients on the label. In the USA, cider or perry that has an alcohol concentration less than 7% ABV is under the jurisdiction of the FDA and therefore requires a list of ingredients and, depending on the size of the business, also requires a nutritional declaration (“Nutrition Facts Table” e.g. a calorific value etc.). Cider or perry that is outside the jurisdiction of the FDA (i.e. has an alcoholic strength above the maximum allowed) does not require an ingredients list but the label MUST be sent to the TTB for approval (“Certificate Of Label Approval” COLA). 6. Allergen Declaration Notwithstanding the above information on ingredients declarations, Sulphur Dioxide / Sulphites at concentrations above 10mg/l MUST always be declared on the label. If the following allergens are also present or used in the production process, they should also normally be declared (current requirements should be checked with the legislators of the individual country concerned):

- Crustaceans or products thereof - Chitin-based fining agents (e.g. Chitosan) – “Contains crustacean

products”

- Fish or products thereof – use of Isinglass or fish gelatin – “Contains fish products”

- Cereals containing gluten and products thereof – use of glucose (dextrose) derived from wheat or barley–

“Contains cereals containing gluten”

- Dairy products – use of casein fining agents – “Contains milk products”

- Egg products – use of albumin fining agents – “Contains egg products”

7. Health Warning In the USA there is a mandatory requirement to include a health warning on all packaged cider or perry. Currently, in many other countries of the world there is no mandatory requirement for such labelling, but it is expected that such a warning is included. 8. Date Marking Usually, unless otherwise required by your customer (e.g. a supermarket), currently cider and perry obtained by fermentation do NOT need to bear a date of minimum durability. There are some exceptions to this e.g. in Canada, if the durable life of a product is 90 days or less, a durable life date must be declared on the label. 9. Traceability There is a general obligation to ensure full traceability (e.g. ingredients used etc) for all products in the marketplace. Organic Cider & Perry: All cider or perry sold as “organic” must be produced according to the laws on organic production in the country in which the product is to be sold. Typically, these laws require products sold as “organic” to come from growers, processors and importers who are registered and approved by organic certification bodies (e.g. Ecocert Canada and CCOF Certification Services, LLC in the USA) who are, in turn, accredited by the country’s own government. Organic certification bodies appoint inspectors who are, for example, expected to visit organic registered cider manufacturing facilities and check that no additives have been used that are not approved for organic production.


## Food Safety


In the food and drink sector the legal obligation for the product to be safe for the consumer has been established for a long time. Typically legal obligations include: o That it is an offence to supply food that fails Safety, Health and Quality requirements. o The food premises must be registered with the appropriate authorities. o There are strong powers of legal enforcement, which may result in improvement notices being issued, closure and prosecution. o There are controls over contaminants and residues. o There is a requirement for staff to undergo hygiene training. o There are a number of specific regulations that must be adhered to. o Notably, in most countries of the world, there is a specific legal obligation for producers to have a food safety management system based on the principles of Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point (HACCP). HACCP is a powerful Quality Assurance technique used to identify hazards with their associated risk and then put into place controls at specific stages of production (Critical Control Points) to ensure no harm to the consumer will result. HACCP Definitions

- A Hazard: Anything that has the potential to cause harm to the consumer

- Risk: An assessment of how likely the Hazard will be realized if not properly controlled AND what and

how severe would be the consequences to consumer safety, health and wellbeing. Hazards are always likely to be present in the cider making process and may be associated with: Equipment design & operation; Building design; Processes used; Personal practice; ingredients / materials used; Product properties. Hazards can be categorised as: 1. Physical (e.g. glass) 2. Chemical (e.g. a toxic chemical) 3. Biological / Microbiological (e.g. a bacterial pathogen) Hazard analysis involves assessing the particular situation to identify hazards, their severity and likelihood of occurrence. The principle defence is: “Due Diligence” In other words, the producer must demonstrate that steps have been taken to identify hazards and undertake risk assessments, with controls then implemented and operated to prevent hazards becoming a reality. Coupled with this, there is a general obligation for the producer to also demonstrate “Good Manufacturing Practice” in their production business.


## Legal Obligations in North America


In Canada, all food operators are responsible under Canadian law for the safety of the food & drink they produce and distribute. Essentially, it is expected that all producers will operate a HACCP system. In the USA, the FDA Food Safety Modernization Act (FSMA) - the most sweeping reform of the food safety laws in more than 70 years - was signed into law on January 4, 2011. It aims to ensure the U.S. food supply is safe by shifting the focus from responding to contamination to preventing it. In 2015, a rule (regulation) was finalised that applies to foods for humans, pets and livestock and requires companies to draw up and implement written plans for keeping food safe. Companies must identify hazards in manufacturing, measures to reduce the risk of contamination and methods to verify that the controls are working – essentially, a HACCP system.
